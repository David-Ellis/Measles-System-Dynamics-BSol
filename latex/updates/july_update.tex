\section{Workshop Summary}

A a participatory measles modelling workshop was held on the 20th of March 2026. This workshop, hosted by Birmingham City Council, brought together local experts to build a conceptual model of the local measles system, to identify behavioural and epidemiological factors that facilitate and block flows in the system, and to identify potential interventions to improve these flows. Attendees to the workshop included employees from Birmingham City Council (7), Birmingham and Solihull Integrated Care Board (2), and Solihull Metropolitan Council (1).

Due to the length of the workshop, Copilot was used to extract vocal contributions to supplement the written materials. The full output, along with all non-sensitive materials related to this project, can be viewed on  \href{https://github.com/David-Ellis/Measles-System-Dynamics-BSol}{GitHub}. 

\subsection{Barriers and Facilitators to Vaccination}

Workshop participants were separated into two groups and given a basic flow diagram for the local measles system with five connected population states: susceptible, vaccinated, infected, hospitalised, and recovered. The groups were asked to identify all the various factors influencing the rate of flow of people from one state to another. These factors were appended to the model at the flow point that they influence. The resulting models have been digitally transcribed and are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:trans-models} as Figures~\ref{fig:group1-model} and \ref{fig:group2-model}. Photographs of the models produced in the workshop can also be found on \href{https://github.com/David-Ellis/Measles-System-Dynamics-BSol}{GitHub}.

Once participants had collated all of the factors they could think of, they were asked to vote on which factors they thought were most important. Each participant was given three stickers which which they could vote on factors. These votes are also transcribed in Figures~\ref{fig:group1-model} and \ref{fig:group2-model}. After the workshop, these factors were grouped together and counted. The total number of vote for each flow factor is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:flow-factors}. 

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/workshop/flow-factors.pdf}
    \caption{Number of participant votes on factors believed to play an important role in influencing parents willingness to vaccinate their children against measles.}
    \label{fig:flow-factors}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:flow-factors}, all of the factors voted on related to vaccination. The factor with the most votes was the accessibility of vaccination ($n=7$), followed by health literacy ($n=6$), the influence and previous experiences of family members ($n=4$), and community norms ($n=4$).

\subsection{Prioritised Interventions}

Participants were asked to review the models they had created, and identify the ``problematic" flows within the system. In their groups, they were asked to brainstorm a list of potential interventions to improve vaccine uptake and which organisations they thought would be responsible for this intervention. These interventions are also shown in Figures~\ref{fig:group1-model} and \ref{fig:group2-model}.

The groups were then asked to each choose the three of interventions that they thought were worth prioritising including in the model. These six interventions were then anonymously ranked by each of the workshop attendees using Mentimeter. The results were then aggregated using a Borda count approach, in which each ranked position was assigned a decreasing number of points and the total score for each option was used to generate the final ordering \cite{borda1784, taylor2005}. The total number of points for each intervention is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:int-voting}.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/workshop/intervention-voting.pdf}
    \caption{Anonymous participant priority ranking of interventions designed to improve measles vaccination uptake. Total score for each intervention calculated using a Borda count approach.}
    \label{fig:int-voting}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:int-voting}, most highly ranked intervention was an extension of the vaccination offer. This intervention, delivered jointly by the NHS and local authorities (LA), would involve expanding the avenues through which children could be vaccinated including community pharmacies (NHS), health visitors (LA), and nurseries and playgroups (NHS/LA).

The second most highly ranked interventions was a local communications campaign to challenge misinformation and improve health literacy. Participants recommended that, while this work would be commissioned by the local authorities and/or the NHS, it would involve local influencers or marketing agencies. Doing so would help to ensure that campaigns are engaging, culturally relevant, and separate from formal government branding. It was noted that social media content that is humorous and relatable tends to outperform formal health messaging and that this should be harnessed in countering misinformation.  

The third most highly ranked intervention was to provide a gelatine-free vaccine as standard. It was agreed that this would improve uptake by reducing confusion and removing the need for families to specifically request the gelatine-free option. This intervention would be led by national government, the Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunisation, and the NHS. 

\section{Basic Modelling}

\subsection{Model tuning}
\label{sec:tuning}
To maximise the use of data, the model is tuned by minimising an error function. As the objective is to reproduce the outbreak dynamics rather than the calendar timing of the outbreak, a time-lag parameter, $\tau$, is included so that simulated outcomes are compared with observed outcomes at the corresponding point in the epidemic. The error function is then given by
%
\begin{equation}
    E_V(p_i, \tau) = \sum_t\left( V_{t+\tau}^{\text{sim}}(p_i) - V_t^{\text{obs}}\right)^2 
    \label{eq:error}
\end{equation}
%
where $p_i$ represents all free model parameters to be tuned, and $V_{t+\tau}^{\text{sim}}$ and $V^{\text{obs}}$ are the simulated and observed measurable values respectively. 

There are two observable values for the model to be tuned to: measles infections and hospital admissions. Therefore, the full error function to be minimised is
%
\begin{equation}
    E(p_i) = \frac{E_I(p_i)}{\mathrm{sd}(E_I)} + \omega \frac{E_H(p_i)}{\mathrm{sd}(E_H)}
    \label{eq:error-comb}
\end{equation}
%
where $E_I$ and $E_H$ are the error functions for infections and hospital admissions respectively and $\omega$ is a weighting parameter that encodes the relative importance of $E_I$ to $E_H$ in the error function calculation. The error functions $E_I$ and $E_H$ are normalised by dividing by their standard deviation across all parameters. Since this investigation is most interested in measles infections \tkDE{(why is this the case?)}, a value of $\omega = 0.5$ was chosen. However, it should be noted that this is a somewhat arbitrary choice and that a range of other values could also be considered appropriate.  

\subsubsection{Data}

Model parameters are tuned to data from large measles outbreak that occurred from late 2023 to 2024. Weekly lab-confirmed cases for Birmingham were extracted from \citeauthor{jary2025sociodemographic} \cite{jary2025sociodemographic}. Weekly case data for Solihull was not available. Therefore, a first order approximation was made using yearly data from the UK Health Security Agency \cite{ukhsa_measles_2023_2026}. Measles-related hospital admissions data were calculated using Secondary User Statistics (SUS) from the Birmingham and Solihull Integrated Care Board (BSol ICB) data warehouse. All dominant hospital episodes with an ICD-10 code starting in 'B05' were included. 

The weekly lab-confirmed cases and hospital admissions are visualised in Figure~\ref{fig:2324-Outbreak}, along with a full explanation of the Solihull case-approximation is in Appendix~\ref{app:cases-approx}. 

\subsection{SIR model}
\label{sec:sir}

In order to first simulate the most basic system behaviour, only three population states were simulated: susceptible (S), infected (I), and recovered (R). It is assumed that these three populations are perfectly mixed and that everyone within the population has an equal probability of contacting each other person.

The three population states are connected by an infection rate determined by the basic reproduction number $R_0$ and the infectious period $1/\gamma$ and recovery rate which is also determined by the infectious period. For this simple model, the transition between states can be described by the following set of ordinary differential equations

\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\diff S}{\diff t} = -\eqnmarkbox[blue]{inf1}{\frac{R_0\gamma SI}{N}}, 
    \qquad 
    \frac{\diff I}{\diff t} = \eqnmarkbox[blue]{inf2}{\frac{R_0\gamma SI}{N}} - \eqnmarkbox[magenta]{rec1}{\gamma I}, 
    \qquad 
    \frac{\diff R}{\diff t} = \eqnmarkbox[magenta]{rec2}{\gamma I}.
    \label{eq:SIR}
\end{equation}
\annotatetwo[yshift=-1em]{below}{inf1}{inf2}{Infection rate}
\annotatetwo[yshift=1em]{above}{rec1}{rec2}{Recovery rate}
\vspace{1.5em}

Since this model does not include births or deaths $N = S + I + R$ is a fixed value. The values for $R_0$, $\gamma$, and $N$ are taken from the literature and are presented in Table~\ref{tab:model-params}. The proportion of the population initially susceptible to measles $S/N$ was taken to be 20\%. This basic SIR model is shown by the blue components of Figure~\ref{fig:SIR+isol}. 

If there exists one or more infected persons ($I>0$), more people will be infected so long as $\diff I / \diff t >0$. Therefore, from Equation~\ref{eq:SIR}, it can be seen that condition for an outbreak for an SIR model is that, 
%
\begin{equation}
    \frac{S}{N} = \frac{1}{R_0}.
\end{equation}
%
This is the SIR condition for herd immunity. For measles, $R_0=13.5$. Therefore, the SIR model predicts that an outbreak will continue until there are fewer than 7.5\% susceptible to infection. 

In reality, measles outbreaks are not left to infect people until herd immunity is achieved through natural infection. Instead, authorities such as the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) intervene to break the spread of transmission by identifying and isolating infected individuals. A model that reproduces observed outcomes therefore requires a mechanism to model this isolation process. 

One of simple ways to model this is to implement an ``isolation mode" that turns on a fixed number of days after the first measles case. Once this mode is activated, a daily fixed proportion of infected persons are moved to an isolated state in which they can no longer infect others. The time delay to response $p_D$ and the isolation proportion $p_P$ are free model parameters. Their values will be tuned to observed data as outlined in Section~\ref{sec:tuning}. When the ``isolation mode" is activated, the rate of change of infected persons is modified to be

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\diff I}{\diff t} \rightarrow \frac{R_0\gamma SI}{N} - \gamma I - \eqnmarkbox[red]{inf3}{p_P I}
    \label{eq:isolation}
\end{equation}
\annotate[yshift=-1em]{below}{inf3}{Isolation}
\vspace{1.5em}

Standard practice for communicable disease is to only consider an outbreak ``over" after two maximum incubation periods occurring without any new cases \cite{UKHSA2026MeaslesGuidelines}. Since the incubation period for measles is 7 to 18 days, the outbreak should be considered ongoing until 36 days have passed without a new case. After this point, authorities can stop looking for infected individuals.

This can be modelled with an ``isolation switch". The mechanics of this switch are demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:switch-model}. If there is at least one person currently infected, and the switch isn't currently on, then after a fixed delay $p_D$ the switch turns ``on" and a fixed proportion of infected persons are isolated each day. However, if there are no infected persons ($I(t)<1$), then the switch will turn off after two maximum incubation periods (36 days).

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[trim=10pt 250pt 480pt 10pt, clip=true, width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/model-diagrams/switch-diagram.pdf}
    \caption{Flow diagram outlining the mechanics of the measles isolation switch.}
    \label{fig:switch-model}
\end{figure}

Since the simulation deals in continuous values rather than discrete units representing individual people, it is necessary to modify the number of new cases to be

\begin{equation}
    \text{Infection rate} = \begin{cases}
        R_0 \gamma S  I/ N, & \text{if } I \geq 1,\\
        0, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
% 
This means that new infections can only occur if the number of people infected is equal to or greater than one. This prevents the unrealistic case of new outbreaks occurring due to less than one infected person. 

The additional simulation components related to this isolation mechanism are presented in green in Figure~\ref{fig:SIR+isol}.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/model-diagrams/SIR+isolation.png}
    \caption{Diagram of a basic SIR system (blue) extended to include the ability to isolate infected individuals (green) at a fixed daily proportion $p_P$. This isolation mode is activated $p_D$ days after the first infection has occurred.}
    \label{fig:SIR+isol}
\end{figure}

When fitting the free parameters of the extended SIR model, the system was initialised with a single infected person. The error function for weekly incidence $E_I$ was then calculated using Equation~\ref{eq:error} for a range of isolation delay $p_D$ and proportion $p_P$ values. From this, the combination of parameters that minimise the error function was calculated.

\subsection{Full model}

The full model, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:full-model}, is an extension of the SIR model with isolation to include three different age groups: babies ($<$ one year), children (1 to 4 years), and "adults" (5+ years), as well as an additional hospitalised population state. This gives a total of 15 different population states (3 age groups $\times$ 5 states). In this model, hospitalised individuals are assumed to also be isolated. Therefore, the addition of this state will change the isolation threshold ($p_T$) and proportion ($p_P$) needed to fit the model to observational data. 

The inclusion of a hospitalised state gives the model provides another output that can be compared and fitted to observed data. It is therefore necessary to include an additional free parameter $p_H$ which controls the rate of at which infected individuals are admitted to hospital. 

Importantly, the full model also includes childhood vaccinations. Vaccinations are modelled as a fixed number of daily children moving to the "recovered" state. This number is taken to be the average number of vaccines given to children aged 1 to 4 years old in BSol between 2021 and 2025 \cite{NHSEngland2026FDP}. It also includes age-specific migration into Birmingham with vaccination proportions estimated by combining \tkDE{migration estimates with WHO vaccination estimates} as outlined in Appendix~\ref{app:migration}. Finally, the model includes age-specific all-cause mortality calculated using data from the deaths register for deaths recorded between 2022 and 2025 \cite{BSolICBWarehouse2026}. This date range was chosen to avoid the impact of the Covid-19 pandemic on the mortality estimates.

\subsubsection{Age-specific rates}

The probability of infection and the probability of hospital admission when infected are age-dependent \cite{jary2025sociodemographic, van2020explaining}. Following the approach of \citeauthor{van2020explaining}, the basic reproduction number can be written as 
%
\begin{equation}
    R_0\gamma = \frac{\alpha_AP_A + \alpha_BP_B + \alpha_CP_C}{P_A + P_B + P_C},
    \label{eq:R0-age}
\end{equation}
%
where $\alpha_i$ and $P_i$ are the relative infection probability and population size respectively for adults (A), babies (B), and children (C) \cite{van2020explaining}.

    \qquad

\end{equation}
%
Substituting into Equation~\ref{eq:R0-age}, it can be therefore be shown that $\alpha_A = 1.27$, $\alpha_B = 2.52$, and $\alpha_c = 6.20$. The age-specific infection rates can then be calculated by making the replacement $R_o \gamma \rightarrow \alpha_i$.

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:hosp-rates}

\end{equation}

However, to ensure that the modelled hospital admissions match the observed numbers, the infected adult daily hospitalisation proportion $h_A$ is left as a free parameter to be fitted.

The error function for weekly incidence $E_I$ and hospital admissions $E_H$ were then calculated using Equation~\ref{eq:error} for a range of values for the isolation delay $p_D$, isolation proportion $p_P$, and adult hospitalisation proportion $p_H$. From this, the combination of parameters that minimise the combined error function was calculated.

\subsection{Outbreak Management}

Within the presented model, outbreak management is controlled by the isolation delay $p_D$ and isolation proportion $p_P$. By varying these values it is possible to estimate how different outbreak management scenarios changes the number of people estimated to be infected and hospitalised during an outbreak. 

Figure~\ref{fig:outbreak-management} shows the number of estimates cases and admissions associated with changes in the isolation delay and proportion parameters from those derived from fitting to data from the 23/24 outbreak shown in Table~\ref{tab:tuned-params}. The isolation delay was varied from 35.5 days (-36\%) to 75 days (+36\%), and the isolation proportion was varied from 0.0763 (-19\%) to 0.150 (60\%). 

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/results/outbreak-response.pdf}
    \caption{Estimates number of cases (left) and hospital admissions (right) with changing isolation delay and proportion parameters compared to the 23/24 outbreak.}
    \label{fig:outbreak-management}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:outbreak-management} shows that a small reduction in isolation proportion could greatly increase the number measles cases. For example, a 20\% decrease in the daily proportion of infected individuals being isolated results in 300.3 (+83.8\%) more cases and 102.6 (+83.6\%) more hospital admissions. However, increasing the isolation proportion provides diminishing returns on improvements in outcomes. For example, a 20\% increase in the daily proportion of infected individuals being isolated results in 88 (-24.5\%) fewer cases and 32 (-24.5\%) fewer hospital admissions.

In contrast, a 20\% reduction in the isolation delay (11.6 days) results in 191.6 (-53.4\%) fewer cases and 65.4 (-53.3\%) fewer admissions. A 20\% increase in the isolation delay, however, results in 378.4 (105.6\%) more cases and 129.2 (105.3\%) more hospital admissions.

This analysis highlights that detecting an outbreak early (encoded in this model via $p_D$) has a greater potential for suppressing the overall outbreak than improving the effectiveness of the case-finding efforts (encoded in this model via $p_P$). This finding is consistent with a 2021 evidence-based review of the effectiveness of contact tracing for the mitigation and suppression of viral diseases, as well as a 2021 systematic review of contact tracing methods for controlling measles outbreaks in low and middle-income countries \cite{craig2021effectiveness, mbivnjo2022measles}. 

%However, this also arises mathematically from the exponential nature of infectious disease. For a simple SIR system with isolation, it follows from Equation.~\ref{eq:isolation} that $I\propto \mathrm{e}^{R_{\mathrm{eff}} - \gamma -p_P}$ where $R_{\rm{eff}} = R_0\gamma SI/N$. The number of infected persons increases so long as the exponent is positive

However, this also arises mathematically from the exponential nature of infectious disease \cite{zhang2024seir}. Consider a single case of measles (basic reproduction number $R_0 = 13.5$) in a fully susceptible population. Each infectious generation increases the number of cases by more than an order of magnitude. After two generations, there are approximately $13.5^2 = 182$ cases. After four generations, there are approximately $13.5^4 = 33,215$ cases. While the \textit{effective} reproduction number is much smaller than 13.5, this highlights how quickly the number of infected persons can increase. Once there are many infectious people, any intervention becomes less effective.

For these reason, the World Health Organisation recommends that to create surveillance system capable of eliminating measles and rubella, the early detection of outbreaks should be given the highest priority \cite{world2024eliminating}.

\subsection{Outbreak Preparation}

Given the tuned model, it is also possible to predict what future measles outbreaks might look like given different levels of child vaccination. Figure~\ref{fig:outbreak-prep} shows the estimated number of measles infections and measles-related hospital admissions after one and five years as a function of changing relative number of MMR(V) vaccinations. For 0\% change, the number of yearly vaccinations is fixed to the average annual number of vaccines given to children aged 1 to 4 years old within BSol between 2021 and 2025. 

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/results/outbreak-prep.pdf}
    \caption{Caption}
    \label{fig:outbreak-prep}
\end{figure}

It is estimated that, at current birth and vaccination rates, the number of people that would be infected in an outbreak reduces over time. After one year the estimated number of measles infections and hospital admissions are 179.6 (78.3, 567.7) and 61.9 (30.9, 158.1) respectively, which are 193.4 (51.8\%) and 62.7 (50.3\%) fewer than the simulated estimate for today (See Table~\ref{tab:summary-stat-comp}). After five years, the estimated number of infections and hospital admissions fall to 42.9 (23.9, 92.8) and 15.2 (9.8, 26.4) respectively. This implies that Birmingham and Solihull is currently on a path towards measles elimination. 

However, Figure~\ref{fig:outbreak-prep} also how the number of estimated infections and hospital admissions rises rapidly with falling yearly MMR(V) vaccinations. If MMR(V) vaccinations are 10\% (\tkDE{X}) or 20\% (\tkDE{X}) then the estimated number of infections after one year are estimated to be 295.9 (115.3, 1174.9) and 517.2 (174.9, 2949.5) respectively, and after five years to be 171.8 (73.7, 551.8) and 1028.1 (285.4, 6815.9) respectively. It can be seen from the larger confidence intervals, that the estimates relating to lower child vaccination rates are much more dependent on the specific model parameters.

\section{Calculating Outcomes from 23/24 Outbreak}
\label{app:cases-approx}

The number of weekly measles cases in Birmingham and the number of weekly hospital admissions (ICD10 code ``B05") in Birmingham and Solihull from 13/10/2023 to 12/04/2024 are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:2324-Outbreak}. Case data for Birmingham was extracted from \citeauthor{jary2025sociodemographic} who defined a case to be ``a Birmingham resident with laboratory confirmation (measles IgM in blood or oral fluid in the absence of recent vaccination (within 14 days) or confirmed wildtype measles RNA in any clinical specimen)" \cite{jary2025sociodemographic}. This definition differs from that used in UKHSA's public measles reports which only includes lab-confirmed cases. This can be seen from the fact that \citeauthor{jary2025sociodemographic} report 175 Birmingham cases in 2023 starting from October 13th, while UKHSA report only 118 for all of 2023 \cite{ukhsa_measles_2023_2026}.

Weekly case-data for Solihull was not available. For 2023, annual data for Solihull was not reported since there were fewer than ten cases. For 2024, UKHSA reported a total of 364 and 16 lab-confirmed cases for Birmingham and Solihull respectively. Due to Solihull's geographical proximity to Birmingham, it is likely that the majority of the cases for the local authority occurred at the same time. If the weekly cases, reported for Birmingham by \citeauthor{jary2025sociodemographic} is $C_{B}$, then we can approximate the weekly cases in Solihull to be
%
\begin{equation}
    C_S = \frac{16C_B}{364}.
\end{equation}

This approximation simultaneously accounts for the reduced reporting period and the additional cases not confirmed by a lab reported by \citeauthor{jary2025sociodemographic}. While this is a crude approximation, since Solihull contributed 4.2\% of the UKHSA-reported cases in 2024, it is unlikely that this will have a significant impact on the fitting of the model to these data.
