\section{Background}
\label{sec:intro}
\tkDE{Measles is a highly infectious viral disease} that can have serious complications and fatality \cite{WHO_measles_topic}. 
In high-income countries, complications occur in around 10-20\% of cases and the case fatality ratio is around 0.05\% in  \cite{wolfson2009estimates, NICE2024MeaslesComplications}. Clinically vulnerable groups, such as immunosuppressed individuals, infants under one year, and pregnant people, are more likely to develop complications \cite{NHSEngland2024MeaslesGuidance}. 

The first measles vaccine was licensed in the US in 1963 \cite{MOSS20172490}. The measles vaccine was first introduced in the UK in 1968, later replaced by the combined measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine in 1988 \cite{ukhsa_vaccination_timeline_1796_present}. As of 1 January 2026, varicella has been added to make the MMRV vaccine. In the UK all children are offered a first dose of MMRV when they turn 12 months and a second dose at 18 months \cite{UKHSA2023MeaslesRubellaElimination}. The World Health Organisation (WHO) estimates that between 2000 and 2024, the measles vaccine has prevented  59 million deaths \cite{mondiale2025weekly}. 

The UK achieved measles elimination in 2016 \cite{UKHSA2023MeaslesRubellaElimination}. However, measles outbreaks are again becoming increasing common in the UK as well as globally due to falling vaccine coverage rates \cite{minta2023progress}. The top half of Figure.~\ref{fig:measles-background} shows the that there has been a series of measles outbreaks in England since January 2012. 

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/background/measles-background.pdf}
    \caption{ (Top) Monthly lab-confirmed cases of measles in England \cite{UKHSA2025MMRLabConfirmedCasesQ3}. (Bottom) Yearly MMR coverage for one dose at two years old \cite{FTips}.}
    \label{fig:measles-background}
\end{figure}


The bottom half of Figure.~\ref{fig:measles-background} shows that these outbreaks have occurred in a context of falling vaccination coverage nationally from a peak of 92.7\% in 2013 down to 88.9\% in 2024.  However, in Birmingham and Solihull (BSol), the  coverage started lower and has fallen further than the England average from a peak of 89.7\% in 2014 down to 81.3\% in 2024 \cite{FTips}. This has contributed to the region suffering large measles outbreaks \cite{jary2025sociodemographic}. \tkDE{Info from Carol Chatt.}

Part of the reason for the falling MMR coverage is the growing scepticism in vaccines \citethis. While this is a trend for most vaccines, there has been particular public concern about the MMR vaccine due to the infamous, since retracted, claim by one research group that the MMR vaccine might cause autism \cite{novilla2023parents, wakefield1998retracted, godlee2011wakefield}. However, there many factors leading to vaccine hesitancy that impact different groups differently including misinformation, health literacy, and lack of trust in the healthcare system \cite{cagnotta2025parental}. Some groups, such as Gypsy, Roma, and Traveller populations, also face additional barriers to accessing the vaccine \cite{wilder2020resurgence}. As a result, significant ethnic and socioeconomic inequality has been observed in MMR coverage \cite{Flatte079550, zhang2023ethnic}. 

During the outbreak in Birmingham from 13 October 2023 the measles infection rate was 3.4 times higher for those living in the most deprived quintile compared to those living in the third most deprived quintile. No infections occurred in the least deprived quintile. Additionally, the infection rate for Black African residents was eight times greater than the white British population \cite{Tabacchi02072016}. Figure.~\ref{fig:BSol-mmr-eth} shows that, except for those of Indian heritage, children off all ethnic groups have lower MMR coverage at one dose than the White British cohort. The Black African and Black Caribbean have particularly low coverage at 74.2\% and 64.0\% respectively compared to 92.9\% for Whit British \cite{NHSEngland2026FDP}.

    \includegraphics[width=0.91\linewidth]{figures/background/mmr-background.pdf}
    \caption{Percentage of children aged less than six years old living in BSol who have received at least one dose of MMR or MMRV as of 04/02/2026 \cite{NHSEngland2026FDP}. The error bars indicate the 95\% CI calculated using the Wilson method.}
    \label{fig:BSol-mmr-eth}
\end{figure}

Among the Core Cities of England and Wales\footnote{England and Wales Core Cities include Birmingham, Bristol, Cardiff, Leeds, Liverpool, Manchester, Newcastle, Nottingham, and Sheffield.}, Birmingham has the highest level of ethnic diversity. Data from the 2021 Census show that just under half (48.5\%) of BSol residents identified as White British, meaning that a majority (51.5\%) reported a different ethnic background. This is almost double the England average of 26.5\%. The largest ethnic minority groups were Pakistani (14.9\%), Indian (5.7\%), Black African (5.0\%), and Other White (3.8\%) \cite{ONS2021census}.


All six regions of the World Health Organization (WHO) have declared measles elimination goals \cite{WHO_measles_topic}. The UK Measles and Rubella elimination strategy 2019 set the MMR vaccination target, based on WHO recommendation, to 95\% of two doses by 5 years of age in order to achieve and maintain measles elimination \cite{public2019uk}. The Public Health England (PHE) immunisation inequalities local action plan template outlines that factors responsible for inequitable vaccine uptake are context specific \cite{PHE2021ImmunisationInequalities}. Therefore, in order to reverse the falling measles vaccine coverage and growing health inequalities, it is therefore imperative that we understand the range of factors influencing parent's decision to vaccinate their children and how these factors are influenced by ethnicity. 

This project aims to build a decision-support system in collaboration with a range of local health protection experts working in BSol. An interactive workshop will be hosted to identify behavioural and epidemiological factors that facilitate and block flows in the system and to identify potential interventions to improve these flows. This model, including selected interventions, will be built using a system dynamics approach. A full timeline for the project is presented in Appendix.~\ref{app:timeline}.

\section{Literature Review}

This project requires a three pronged literature review. Firstly, a surface-level review will be conducted into the primary computational methods that have used to simulate epidemics including vaccinations. This review will assess the strengths and weaknesses of each method and to what extent they have been applied to address inequalities in measles vaccination. Reviews will be also be conducted on the factors that influence parents decisions to vaccinate their children as well as a review of interventions that have been conducted to increase childhood vaccinations. These last two reviews will compiled into an evidence pack that will be provided to stakeholders to support the participatory modelling workshop. 

A semi-structured approach will be adopted to finding literature. Literature will be sourced from PubMed and Proquest and supplemented with additional papers found through other means. Local intelligence will also be identified through consultation with health protection leads at BCC and Solihull Metropolitan Council. All three reviews will consider only English language papers published in the last 5 years for simulation methods and the last 10 years for vaccination barriers and interventions (2016 or more recent).

\subsubsection{SEIR Models}

As shown in Table.~\ref{tab:modelling-papers}, the majority of the identified measles simulations used a susceptible, exposed, infectious, recovered (SEIR) model or some variant thereof \cite{peter2026assessing, chukwu2026forecasting, peter2025modelling, liu2025modelling, sinigirira2025assessing,  farhan2025fractional, fahimi2025mathematical, khan2025deterministic, sbarra2025fitting, robert2024long, rasit2024mathematical, berhe2024modelling, ibrahim2024threshold, chatterjee2024compartmental, kuddus2023analysis, ibrahim2023stability, farman2023modelling, alemneh2023modelling, pokharel2022modeling, el2022mathematical, shen2022impact, Kuddus2021, MENKIR20212894}. These models compartmentalise a population into distinct states with defined rates of transition from one state to another as outlined in a simple example in Figure.~\ref{fig:SEIR-diagram}. The dynamics of this model can be described by a series by ordinary differential equations (ODEs). The ODEs for the system shown in Figure.~\ref{fig:SEIR-diagram} are outlined in Appendix.~\ref{app:seir-details} including an explanation of basic approaches to solving the equations computationally to calculate the change in population in each state over time. Model parameters such as the infection rate $\beta$, transition rate $\sigma$, and recovery rate $\gamma$, can be drawn from existing literature or estimated by fitting the model to historic data as done by \citeauthor{liu2025modelling} and \citeauthor{chukwu2026forecasting} with strong agreement to the historical data \cite{liu2025modelling, chukwu2026forecasting}.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figures/background/SEIR_model.png}
    \caption{Example of a basic susceptible, exposed, infectious, recovered (SEIR) model, adapted from \cite{bjornstad2020seirs}. The ODEs for the system are outlined in Appendix.~\ref{app:seir-details}.}
    \label{fig:SEIR-diagram}
\end{figure}

Most of the SEIR-type simulations identified include at least one vaccinated state. Doing so, enables future measles infection rates to be estimated for different vaccination scenarios. For example, \citeauthor{sinigirira2025assessing} consider the impact of varying the proportion of people being vaccinated as part of both the routine vaccination programme and an additional vaccination campaign on the number of measles infections in Nigeria \cite{sinigirira2025assessing}.

One weakness of SEIR-type simulations is the inherent assumption of an idealised susceptible population with perfect population mixing \cite{bjornstad2020seirs}. This can be partially overcome by introducing additional states so that different parts of the population can be modelled with different behaviour and parameters. For example, influenza has the biggest health impact on older adults while younger people play a more significant role in spreading the infection \cite{InfluenzaGreenBook2023}. These, age groups therefore need to be modelled separately as done by \citeauthor{NextGenFluModelling} \cite{NextGenFluModelling}. While measles affects people of all ages, it is most dangerous for small children \cite{bassetti2025epidemiology}. Therefore SEIR models of measles often do not model age groups separately. However, as outlined in Section.~\ref{sec:intro}, infection rates can vary significantly across social deprivation. For that reason, \citeauthor{MENKIR20212894} built a transmission model stratified by income \cite{MENKIR20212894}. This enabled the equity of different vaccination coverage strategies to be studied. One of the most challenging aspects of modelling different population groups is estimating the level of epidemiological mixing between each group.

\subsubsection{System Dynamics}

Two of the identified papers published in the last five years used a system dynamics approach \cite{zadeh2025public, Decouttere2021} as well as additional paper outside the given publication window \cite{van2020explaining}. 

Like standard SEIR models, system dynamics modelling is used to model the behaviour of complex non-linear systems. In system dynamics terminology, these systems are represented using stocks, flows, and feedback loops \cite{bala2017system}. The primary difference between these approaches is in the modelling philosophy. While SEIR systems are used to describe disease transmission dynamics, system dynamics is used to understand how the interactions between different system components generate behaviour over time. As such, system dynamics modelling often includes more abstract and difficult-to-quantify factors, including information flows and decision processes.

System dynamics has been often used in the planning of primary and secondary healthcare systems but typically been used less often for modelling infectious disease transmission \cite{davahli2020system}. A minimal system dynamics model for infectious disease transmission modified from \cite{van2020explaining} is presented in Figure.~\ref{fig:SD-model-example}. The boxes indicates the \textit{stocks} (segments of the population), connected by \textit{flows} in solid gray. The red lines indicate the flow of information. The clouds indicates points where the population enters (births) or leaves (deaths) the system being modelled.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/background/SD_model.png}
    \caption{Minimal system dynamics model for infectious disease transmission modified from \cite{van2020explaining}. \tkDE{Add loop labels if time.}}
    \label{fig:SD-model-example}
\end{figure}

The earliest identified paper on simulating measles vaccinations and infections using system dynamics by \citeauthor{van2020explaining} presents a "stylised" model of the measles system including epidemic/medical and behavioural factors. This model is developed as to capture the key dynamics of the system and to facilitate a conceptual understanding of the system and the interactions between different factors, rather than to produce accurate epidemiological predictions of future infection numbers \cite{van2020explaining}. Much like the SEIR models discussed, \citeauthor{van2020explaining} segment the population into those who are susceptible, infected, recovered and vaccinated, as well as those who experienced severe side effects of vaccination and those who recovered from measles but with severe long-term impacts. These population states are connected by epidemiological factors such as infection rate and recovery rate, and behavioural science factors such perceived risk of the disease and, conversely, the perceived risk of the vaccine. These factors, both influenced by the number of people infected and vaccinated with side effects, as well as the impact of external information, influence parents willingness to vaccinate and therefore the overall vaccination rate \cite{van2020explaining}. Doing so, \citeauthor{van2020explaining} simulate the long-term (80 years) impact of a series of 10 scenarios relating to different vaccination campaigns and minor and major vaccine scares like the 1998 Wakefield scandal \cite{wakefield1998retracted}. 

Although not directly referencing \citeauthor{van2020explaining}, \citeauthor{Decouttere2021} build on this work to include additional factors including outreach campaigns, community health workers, and physical access to vaccination tailored specifically to the context of Rwanda \cite{Decouttere2021}. However, this was only a conceptual model and not developed into a computational simulation. 

\citeauthor{zadeh2025public}, in the most recent of the identified papers, attempt to create a much more complete disease transmission model including the time from exposure to being infectious, the quarantine of exposed and infected individuals, and international travel \cite{zadeh2025public}. While the authors developed this model to be applicable to any infectious disease, they demonstrated the capabilities of the model for the test case of measles, fitting to data from WHO and Centers for Disease Control to predict the impact of a series of different interventions. 

To the author's knowledge, there has not been a attempt to simulate a measles vaccination system that considers the interaction between epidemiological and behavioural factors including the variation in these factors across ethnicity.  

\subsubsection{Other Methods}

System dynamics and SEIR models are deterministic - given the same input parameters, the outputs will be the same. In contrast, \citeauthor{anwar2025stochastic} implemented a stochastic (probabilistic) model. The resulting data were used to train a neural network to approximate the dynamics of the system \cite{anwar2025stochastic}. Such networks are typically black-box models making the internal relationships between variables difficult to interpret, thus reducing the conceptual transparency and therefore its usefulness as a decision support tool \cite{burkart2021survey}.

The methods considered above all model non-linear behaviour, however, it is also possible to develop linear models. \citeauthor{rodriguez2025heuristic}, present a such a model to optimise the location of vaccination sites, the schedules of medical teams, and the routes taken between sites \cite{rodriguez2025heuristic}. The authors implement a heuristic algorithm to reduce the computational cost of performing the optimisation. Due to its focus on operational systems, this approach is not appropriate for the current project. 

Other important modelling approaches used to simulate the transmission of infectious disease, although not identified in this search include Agent-Based Models  \cite{willem2017lessons} Network Models  \cite{keeling2005networks}. These approaches focus on individual interactions and contact networks, whereas the present study focuses on population-level vaccination dynamics.

The following literature search string was used to identify publications on barriers experienced by parents or carers to have vaccinate their child with the MMR vaccine:

\begin{verbatim}
      (vaccine OR vaccination OR immunisation)
      AND (barrier OR hesitancy)
      AND (child OR parent) 
      AND (measles OR MMR)
\end{verbatim}

There were 16 and 35 publication identified by PubMed and ProQuest respectively. Five additional publications were included, four of these were gray literature based on local community engagement workshops \cite{smbc_community_engagement_learning_2025, kemishi_uasc_health_wellbeing_2025, palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024, bissett_measles_soft_intelligence_2024}. The publications included are presented in Table.~\ref{tab:vaccine-hesitancy-papers}. Due to their increased relevance to the present work, the following review will pay particular focus on the local evidence. 

% Vaccine hesitancy review
One 2024 meta-analysis estimated the global prevalence of care-giver vaccine hesitancy to be 32\% but only 2\% for England \cite{maamor2024prevalence}. This England estimate, based on a small sample taken in 2015, does not seem consistent with the current level of MMR coverage \cite{CAMPBELL20172979}. Another 2017 systematic review of socioeconomic differences in childhood vaccinations found MMR uptake in England to be lower amongst more affluent parents \cite{bocquier2017socioeconomic}. Again, the most recent study this finding was based on was published in 2011. In contrast, ONS data shows that MMR uptake in the least deprived most deprived local authorities (92.0\% compared to 84.3\% respectively) \cite{FTips}. 


% Local evidence

Community engagement workshops conducted Birmingham and Solihull targetted at low-uptake ethnic groups found similar overarching themes \cite{bissett_measles_soft_intelligence_2024, palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024, smbc_community_engagement_learning_2025}. Language barriers and low reading ability arose as a key issue. Many parents in BSol speak little English and therefore struggle to benefit from English-only flyers. Some parents are unable to read in any language. This highlights the importance of using multiple media forms including video for outreach \cite{palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024, smbc_community_engagement_learning_2025}. Participants also reported that the materials used to promote vaccination lacked diversity and did not represent the communities they were from \cite{palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024}. This finding mirrors that of a 2024 systematic review on the barriers and enablers to MMR uptake among disadvantaged, minority, or underserved populations \cite{essa2024understanding}.

Concerns about the ingredients and safety of the vaccine varied by community. For example, Muslim participants were very concerned about pork gelatine and the option of a pork free vaccine \cite{palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024, smbc_community_engagement_learning_2025}. This again mirrors global research where the most commonly cited religiously motivated barriers to measles vaccination were animal derived gelatins used in the vaccine production and the used of aborted human fetus tissue to make the rubella component of the MMR vaccine \cite{wombwell2015religious}.

Additionally, for Muslim/Afro-Caribbean communities, fears about possible links to autism were also seen to be important, while these were less important for the Romani traveller community \cite{palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024, smbc_community_engagement_learning_2025}. This diversity in opinion is important as a 2023 systematic review found the risk of autism to be the most commonly cited concern about the vaccine \cite{novilla2023parents}.

There were also issues raised about the accessibility of the vaccine services, particularly from migrants and asylum seekers
\cite{bissett_measles_soft_intelligence_2024}. A survey of support staff working with unaccompanied asylum seeking children (UASC) living in Birmingham found that UASC often arrive with missing vaccination records that there often isn't enough time within appointments to give the required immunisations \cite{kemishi_uasc_health_wellbeing_2025}. Additionally, a 2025 review of found that many migrant living in Europe don't know how to access vaccine services and don't know what they're eligible for, including fears of costs \cite{halliday2025vaccine}. This is a particularly unnecessary barrier since all migrants in the UK, including those with No Recourse to Public Funds (NRPF) are eligible for free vaccinations on the NHS \cite{nrpf_nhs_treatment_overview, ukhsa_migrant_immunisation_2022}. The same review found that first generation immigrants had low vaccination rates and high levels of unknown vaccination status, reflecting local evidence \cite{halliday2025vaccine}. In particular, one 2020 Welsh study found that asylum seeking children had a 3 times reduced odds to be vaccinated compared to the general population \cite{perry2020inequalities}.

Participants in the Birmingham engagement workshops also raised the issue of trust \cite{bissett_measles_soft_intelligence_2024, palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024}. Some participants reported a general mistrust of government sources, particularly if associated with the 'Home Office' and 'immigration'. A general preference was raised for information to come from healthcare professionals and local community/faith leaders. A lack of trust in the healthcare system has also been found to be a barrier to vaccination in the UK \cite{torracinta2021mmr}. 

Other general reasons for vaccine hesitancy include a belief that children are given too many vaccines too soon, and a low perceived benefit of vaccination \cite{novilla2023parents}. A 2025 cross-sectional study in Türkiye found parents were 2.5 times more likely to exhibit vaccine hesitancy if one of their children had already experienced adverse effects \cite{komurluouglu2025vaccine}.

\section{Research Objectives}

This project will aim to answer the following research question: How can a system dynamics model be used to facilitate the development of interventions to increase measles vaccination rates and reduce negative outcomes, particularly amongst lower-uptake ethnic groups?

The research aims are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Conduct a participatory modelling workshop with expert stakeholders to design a theoretical model for measles vaccinations and outcomes in Birmingham and Solihull.
    \item Identify and prioritise key interventions to be modelled
    \item Develop a functional model and calibrate using historical vaccination, notification, and hospitalisation data
    \item Integrate most highly prioritised interventions to simulate the expected impact on the modelled behavioural science factors, vaccination rates, and health outcomes
\end{itemize}

Long term, the aim is to develop a dashboard for health protection teams working in the system to be able to dynamically plan and test interventions using variable model assumptions. However, this is beyond the scope of the thesis.

\section{SEIR Example}
\label{app:seir-details}

Let S(t), E(t), I(t), A(t) and R(t) be the number of individuals at time t who are susceptible, exposed, infective, and recovered respectively. Since the total population is not fixed, we have \( S(t) + E(t) + I(t) + A(t)+ R(t) = N(t) \) for \( t = \{t_1, \ldots, t_k\} \). 

Individuals in the Susceptible (S) compartment can become Exposed (E) through contact with Infectious (I) individuals at a constant rate $\beta$. Susceptible individuals can also die from all-cause mortality at a constant rate $\mu_d$, become immune through vaccination at a rate $\nu$, or revert to susceptibility from the Recovered (R) state at a rate 
$\omega$. The birth rate $mu_b$ contributes to the influx of new Susceptible individuals into the population. Exposed individuals move to the Infectious state at a rate of $\sigma$ ,which represents the latency period of the infection. Exposed individuals can also die from all-cause mortality at a constant rate $\mu_d$. Infectious individuals recover and move to the Recovered state at a rate $\gamma$, or they may die due to the infection at a rate $\alpha$, in addition to the all-cause mortality $\mu_d$. Infectious individuals can develop acute poliomyelitis which leads to hospital admission at a rate $\eta$. Since hospital admission is not hospitalisation, for simplicity, we assume these individuals leave the compartment immediately in the subsequent time to the recovered compartment.
Recovered individuals can lose their immunity and move back to the Susceptible state at a rate $\omega$, or they may die from all-cause mortality $\mu_d$. Vaccinated individuals (transitioned from Susceptible to Recovered due to vaccination at a rate $\nu$) also follow these dynamics. The model outlines the changes in different state-spaces of the population over time \( t \). In this model, deaths are  depicted as transitions from the \( S \), \( E \), \( I \), or \( R \) compartments to an external state out of the system. 

These movements between states at time $t$ can be expressed by the following differential equations:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\diff S}{\diff t} = \mu_b N - \frac{\beta I S}{N} - \mu_d S + \omega R  - \nu S,
    \label{eq:ODE1}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\diff E}{\diff t} = \frac{\beta I S}{N} - \sigma E - \mu_d E,
    \label{eq:ODE2}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\diff I}{\diff t} = \sigma E - \gamma I - (\mu_d + \alpha) I - \eta I,
    \label{eq:ODE3}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\diff A}{\diff t} = \eta I - A,
    \label{eq:ODE4}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\diff R}{\diff t} = \gamma I - \mu_d R - \omega R + \nu S + A,
    \label{eq:ODE5}
\end{equation}

These equations can then be solved computationally to calculate the change in population in each state over time. The simplest technique is the Euler Method. This method assumes that derivatives can be approximated as constant over small time periods \cite{Butcher2008}. From this a value $Y$ at time step $t + \Delta t$ can be approximated as
\begin{equation}
    Y(t+\Delta t) = Y(t) + \frac{\diff Y(t)}{\diff t}\Delta t.
\end{equation}
%
Following this approach, Eqs.~\ref{eq:ODE1}-\ref{eq:ODE5} can be combined into a single matrix multiplication equation.
