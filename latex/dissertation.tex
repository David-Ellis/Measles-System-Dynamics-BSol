\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Measles Epidemiology}

% Transmission and infectiousness
Measles is a highly infectious viral disease that can cause serious complications and death \cite{WHO_measles_topic}. Measles is primarily transmitted through the respiratory route, including through airborne transmission from breathing contaminated air. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the challenges associated with controlling respiratory infections transmitted through the air, particularly in crowded and poorly ventilated indoor environments \cite{who_covid_ventilation_2021}. As with COVID-19, transmission before the onset of symptoms presents challenges for controlling measles. For measles, infected persons are individuals typically infectious from around four days before until four days after rash onset \cite{WHO_measles_topic}. Additionally, with a basic reproduction number $R_0$ often cited as being between 12 and 18, measles is considerably more transmissible than most respiratory infections and therefore presents an substantial challenge to population-level control \cite{alimohamadi2020estimate, guerra2017basic}.  

% Complications and hospitalisation
A significant proportion of those infected with measles will develop complications and require hospital care. Potential complications from the disease include blindness, encephalitis, severe diarrhoea, ear infections, and breathing problems including pneumonia. Complications from measles infection can also be fatal with an estimated 95,000 people globally dying from measles in 2024. Most of these deaths occurred in low-income countries with weak health infrastructures \cite{WHO_measles_topic}. However, measles complications still pose a significant challenge in high-income countries where complications occur in around 10-20\% of cases, and the case-fatality ratio is around 0.05\% \cite{wolfson2009estimates, NICE2024MeaslesComplications}. Clinically vulnerable groups, such as immunosuppressed individuals, infants under one year, and pregnant people, are more likely to develop complications \cite{NHSEngland2024MeaslesGuidance}.

Immunity to measles can be obtained either following infection or vaccination. Infants can also obtain temporary maternal immunity from their mothers \cite{TILEY2025106531}. Herd immunity is achieved when the proportion of the population immune to a disease is high enough to reduce transmission to a level at which sustained spread cannot be maintained. Assuming homogeneous mixing of completely immune individuals within a population, the critical proportion of the required for herd immunity is approximately $1 - 1/R_0$. Therefore, more infectious diseases require a higher proportion of the population to be immune. 

The measles vaccine was first introduced in the UK in 1968 and later replaced by the combined measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine in 1988 \cite{ukhsa_vaccination_timeline_1796_present}. The MMR vaccine is highly effective, with an estimated effectiveness of 92\% (95\% Confidence interval [CI], 67\%–98\%) after one dose and 95\% (95\% CI, 82\%–98\%) after two doses \cite{UKHSA2026MeaslesGuidelines, 10.1086/498902}. To interrupt measles transmission and maintain elimination, WHO recommends achieving and sustaining at least 95\% coverage with two doses of measles-containing vaccine in every district \cite{who_measles_position_2017}. However, substantial heterogeneity in vaccine uptake, and hence infections, has been observed between population groups within the same country/region \cite{who_subnational_immunization_coverage}. Therefore, achieving a high overall vaccine coverage may not be sufficient to suppress measles transmission and protect the whole population. 

%The World Health Organisation (WHO) estimates that between 2000 and 2024, the measles vaccine has prevented 59 million deaths globally \cite{mondiale2025weekly}. 

\subsection{Measles in England and BSol}

% Impact of a measles outbreak on a UK children's emergency department and the public health response: a retrospective observational study https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0195670125002361

From 1 January 2026, varicella vaccine has been added to the routine childhood immunisation programme as part of the combined MMRV vaccine \cite{nhse_ukhsa_mmrv_2025}. For brevity, both MMR and MMRV will simply be referred to as MMR. Under the current UK vaccination schedule, all children are offered a first dose of MMR when they turn 12 months and a second dose at 18 months \cite{UKHSA2023MeaslesRubellaElimination}. The UK achieved measles elimination in 2016 \cite{UKHSA2023MeaslesRubellaElimination}. However, measles cases and outbreaks have increased in the UK and globally in recent years, coinciding with declines in vaccination coverage \cite{minta2023progress}. The top half of Figure~\ref{fig:measles-background} shows that there has been a series of measles outbreaks in England since January 2012.

The bottom half of Figure~\ref{fig:measles-background} shows these outbreaks occurring alongside falling national vaccination coverage from a peak of 92.7\% in 2013 down to 88.9\% in 2024.  However, in \bsol, the  coverage started lower and has fallen further than the England average from a peak of 89.7\% in 2014 down to 81.3\% in 2024 \cite{FTips}. This decline has left local and national coverage substantially below the 95\% level required to maintain measles elimination and has coincided with substantial measles outbreaks in the \bsol \cite{jary2025sociodemographic}.

The decline in MMR(V) coverage has been attributed to a range of factors, including vaccine hesitancy, misinformation, health literacy, and trust in healthcare systems \cite{gschwend2026shadow, torracinta2021mmr, novilla2023parents}. These factors, alongside barriers to accessing vaccination, can vary between population groups and contribute to inequalities in coverage \cite{cagnotta2025parental}. Consequently, substantial ethnic and socioeconomic inequalities have been observed in MMR coverage \cite{Flatte079550, zhang2023ethnic}.

During the outbreak in Birmingham from 13 October 2023, the measles infection rate was 3.4 times higher for those living in the most deprived quintile compared to those living in the third deprivation quintile. No infections were recorded amongst residents living in the least deprived quintile. Additionally, the infection rate for Black African residents was eight times greater than the White British population \cite{Tabacchi02072016}. Figure~\ref{fig:BSol-mmr-eth} shows that, except for Indian and Chinese children, all ethnic groups have lower MMR coverage at one dose than the White British cohort. Coverage among Black African and Black Caribbean children was particularly low at 74.2\% and 64.0\% respectively compared to 92.9\% for White British \cite{NHSEngland2026FDP}.

Among the Core Cities of England and Wales, Birmingham has the lowest MMR(V) coverage at two years of age\footnote{England and Wales Core Cities include Birmingham, Bristol, Cardiff, Leeds, Liverpool, Manchester, Newcastle, Nottingham, and Sheffield.} \cite{FTips, PHW2024COVER150}. It is also the most ethnically diverse of these cities. Data from the 2021 Census show that just under half (48.5\%) of \bsol residents identified as White British, with the remaining 51.5\% identifying with other ethnic groups. This is almost double the England average of 26.5\%. The largest ethnic minority groups were Pakistani (14.9\%), Indian (5.7\%), Black African (5.0\%), and Other White (3.8\%) \cite{ONS2021census}.

The Public Health England (PHE) immunisation inequalities local action plan template outlines that factors responsible for inequitable vaccine uptake are context-specific \cite{PHE2021ImmunisationInequalities}. Understanding the factors influencing parents' decisions to vaccinate their children, and how these factors vary between population groups, may therefore help inform interventions to improve coverage and reduce inequalities in measles-related health outcomes.

\subsection{Literature Review}
\label{sec:vaccine-barriers}

A rapid review of the literature was undertaken to identify barriers associated with childhood measles vaccination uptake, with particular attention to barriers affecting disadvantaged and minority populations\footnote{The literature search strategy and full literature list can be found in the dissertation protocol presented on the project's \href{https://github.com/David-Ellis/Measles-System-Dynamics-BSol/blob/main/pdfs/Measles\%20System\%20Dynamics\%20Project\%20Protocol.pdf}{GitHub repository}.}. A written summary of the findings was provided to stakeholders before the workshop to establish a shared evidence base, complement their existing knowledge, and inform their contributions to the development of the measles system model.

% Vaccine hesitancy review
Evidence regarding the socioeconomic determinants of childhood MMR uptake in England is relatively limited and often based on older data. A 2017 systematic review of socioeconomic differences in childhood vaccinations found MMR uptake in England to be lower amongst parents with a higher ``socioeconomic status'' (SES) \cite{bocquier2017socioeconomic}. However, the evidence underlying this finding was limited and relatively old, with the most recent contributing study published in 2011, and results varied according to how socioeconomic status was measured \cite{reading2004infant, Pearce754, brown2008attitudinal, brown2011attitudinal, braveman2005socioeconomic}. In contrast, ONS data shows that MMR uptake is lowest in the most deprived local authorities based on the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) \cite{FTips, imd2019}. These more recent population-level data likely provide a better indication of the socioeconomic trends in MMR uptake in England.

% Local evidence
Local community engagement undertaken in Birmingham and Solihull with ethnic groups experiencing low vaccination uptake identified several overlapping themes \cite{bissett_measles_soft_intelligence_2024, palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024, smbc_community_engagement_learning_2025}. Language barriers and limited literacy arose as a key issue. Many parents in BSol speak little English and therefore struggle to benefit from English-only flyers while others are unable to read in any language. This highlights the importance of using multiple media forms, including video, for outreach \cite{palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024, smbc_community_engagement_learning_2025}. Participants also reported that the materials used to promote vaccination lacked diversity and did not represent the communities they were from \cite{palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024}. This finding mirrors that of a 2024 systematic review on the barriers and enablers to MMR uptake among disadvantaged, minority, or underserved populations \cite{essa2024understanding}.

Concerns about the ingredients and safety of the vaccine varied by community. For example, Muslim participants were particularly concerned about pork gelatine and the option of a pork-free vaccine \cite{palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024, smbc_community_engagement_learning_2025}. Globally, religiously motivated concerns about measles vaccination have included the use of animal-derived gelatine and, in some contexts, the use of human foetal cell lines in vaccine production \cite{wombwell2015religious}.

Concerns about a possible link between MMR vaccination and autism were reported as important for some Muslim/Afro-Caribbean participants, although these fears were less prominant for the Romani traveller community \cite{palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024, smbc_community_engagement_learning_2025}. This local diversity in opinion is important as a 2023 systematic review found the risk of autism to be the most commonly cited concern about the vaccine \cite{novilla2023parents}. These fears originate in the now-discredited and retracted claim linking MMR vaccination with autism \cite{wakefield1998retracted, godlee2011wakefield, gagnon2023science, madsen2002population}.

Accessibility was also identified as a barrier, particularly among migrants and asylum seekers \cite{bissett_measles_soft_intelligence_2024}. A survey of support staff working with unaccompanied asylum-seeking children (UASC) living in Birmingham found that UASC often arrive with missing vaccination records, and that there often isn’t enough time within appointments to give the required immunisations \cite{kemishi_uasc_health_wellbeing_2025}. Additionally, a 2025 review found that many migrants living in Europe don’t know how to access vaccine services and don’t know what they’re eligible for, including fears of costs \cite{halliday2025vaccine}. This is notable because all migrants in the UK, including those with No Recourse to Public Funds (NRPF), are eligible for free vaccinations on the NHS \cite{nrpf_nhs_treatment_overview, ukhsa_migrant_immunisation_2022}. The same review found that first-generation immigrants had low vaccination rates and high levels of unknown vaccination status, reflecting local evidence \cite{halliday2025vaccine}. A 2019 systematic review of parental attitudes and beliefs of the measles vaccine also found that parents from Gypsy, Roma, and Traveller (GRT) populations faced unique accessibility barriers due to their transient residency as well as regular discrimination and exclusion within the healthcare system \cite{wilder2020resurgence}.

Participants in the Birmingham engagement workshops also raised the issue of trust \cite{bissett_measles_soft_intelligence_2024, palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024}. Some participants reported a general mistrust of government sources, particularly if associated with the ‘Home Office’ and ‘immigration’. A general preference was raised for information to come from healthcare professionals and local community/faith leaders. A lack of trust in the healthcare system has also been found to be a barrier to vaccination in the UK \cite{torracinta2021mmr}.

Finally, broader perceptions of vaccination may also influence uptake, including concerns about receiving multiple vaccines at a young age, low perceived benefits of vaccination, and previous adverse experiences \cite{novilla2023parents, komurluouglu2025vaccine}.

\subsection{Rationale and Aims}

The evidence outlined above suggests that parents' willingness to have their children vaccinated against measles is influenced by a complex combination of systemic, sociological, and psychological factors. The combined impact of these factors may produce non-linear behaviour within the measles system, including feedback loops and delays, which can be challenging to represent using simpler linear or compartmental approaches \cite{bjornstad2020seirs, rodriguez2025heuristic}. Some methods, including neural networks, can model complex non-linear behaviour but may act as a ``black box'' and provide less insight into how and why the system behaves as it does \cite{anwar2025stochastic, burkart2021survey}. In contrast, system dynamics provides a modelling framework that allows for the study of complex systems including the non-linear interactions between different system elements \cite{bala2017system}. System dynamics can also represent comparatively abstract and difficult-to-quantify factors such as ``trust in the vaccine'' and ``level of health literacy'' \cite{van2020explaining}. Therefore, due to its flexibility and ability to study behavioural factors, a system dynamics approach will be used.

This dissertation presents a snapshot of an ongoing piece of work led by \bcc in collaboration with stakeholders from \solihull, \bsolicb, and \ukhsa. The findings from this project will contribute to future work packages for the Birmingham and Solihull Measles Elimination Group \cite{ukhsa_measles_briefing_2026}.

The overarching project aim is to develop a system dynamics model to support decision-making in collaboration with a range of local health protection experts working in BSol. The intended full model will incorporate ethnicity-dependent behavioural and epidemiological factors, identified by stakeholders through a series of interactive workshops, that facilitate and block flows in the system. The model will also simulate potential interventions to improve these flows. 

This dissertation presents the results from the first interactive workshop including most important factors blocking and facilitating parents' willingness to vaccinate their children and the interventions that stakeholders prioritised for further investigation as potential responses to declining childhood MMR vaccination coverage in \bsol. The development of the base measles model. This is an age-dependent model of measles transmission, vaccination, and hospital admission. Preliminary predictions on the impact of modified outbreak preparation and response are presented. 

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Stakeholder Engagement}

Due to the complicated and multifaceted nature of the measles system, stakeholders with expertise of different aspects of the system were invited to participate in a participatory modelling workshop. Organisations were invited to nominate delegates to participate. These organisations included \bsolicb, \ukhsa, and the public health teams from \bcc, and \solihull. Attempts were also made to ensure that a range of skill sets were present at the workshop including measles case management, child vaccinations, data analytics, and behavioural science. 

To promote full participation, stakeholders were divided into two groups, with group composition selected to provide a broad range of backgrounds and expertise. A simplified version of the stakeholder engagement approach used by \citeauthor{michail2025evaluation} was employed \cite{michail2025evaluation}.

Prior to the workshop, participants were provided with an evidence pack\footnote{This evidence pack can be viewed on the project's \href{https://github.com/David-Ellis/Measles-System-Dynamics-BSol/blob/main/pdfs/Measles\%20System\%20Dynamics\%20Project\%20Protocol.pdf}{GitHub repository}.} outlining the rationale for using system dynamics and summarising local evidence on the systemic, sociological, and psychological barriers to childhood vaccination described in Section~\ref{sec:vaccine-barriers}. The workshop was held at Birmingham City Council House and was facilitated with support from the BCC Governance Team. Participant contributions were recorded using paper and sticky notes, with all materials photographed and subsequently transcribed.

The workshop was divided into three parts. First, participants were given a high-level overview of the evidence pack and an introduction to system dynamics. A practical model-building exercise using flows of money into and out of a bank account was then used to introduce the core concepts of system dynamics.

Second, participants were presented with a simplified measles model showing the principal stocks and flows within the system. For each key flow, participants identified factors that could facilitate or block the flow using sticky notes. Participants then voted on the factors they considered most influential using a limited number of stickers. This was used to focus subsequent model development on factors considered most influential to system behaviour.

Finally, participants identified flows that they considered could be improved and proposed potential policies and interventions to alter these flows. For each intervention, participants identified the organisation responsible, the location within the system at which it would act, and the expected direction of its effect on the relevant flow. These contributions were also recorded using sticky notes. Participants subsequently used an anonymous online voting platform to rank the proposed interventions according to their perceived feasibility and impactability. The resulting prioritisation was used to identify the interventions on which subsequent modelling would focus.

Working transparently with stakeholders is key to developing and maintaining trust and respect with the stakeholders and improves the overall legitimacy of the study \cite{ray2017strengthening}. Therefore, all materials from the workshop were digitally transcribed, results from participant voting were visualised, and presented back to stakeholders for approval and validation. Additionally, all group discussion activities were automatically transcribed using Microsoft Teams. Due to the workshop's length, Copilot was used to summarise this transcription. This summary was also presented back to the stakeholders to allow for accuracy checking. 

\subsection{Modelling}

This study builds on the system dynamics model developed by \citeauthor{van2020explaining}, which models measles vaccination, infection, and mortality for two age groups (children and adults) \cite{van2020explaining}. The present model retains these components while also modelling hospital admissions and the isolation of infected individuals during outbreaks, and disaggregates the population into three age groups (babies, children, and adults). A simple SIR model was first developed to establish the basic transmission dynamics and provide a foundation for subsequent extensions.

System dynamics modelling was conducted using Stella Architect \cite{StellaArchitect}, a proprietary software package with a graphical modelling interface. The visual representation of the model structure facilitated engagement with stakeholders without technical modelling expertise during model development. This helps to engage stakeholders without technical expertise in the model building process. Hospital admission data were extracted using SQL Server Management Studio (version 19.3.4). All data processing and visualisation were performed in R (version 4.3.3). All code and Stella Architect models used in this study are publicly available on \href{https://github.com/David-Ellis/Measles-System-Dynamics-BSol}{GitHub}.

\subsubsection{SIR model}
\label{sec:sir}

To first simulate the most basic system behaviour, only three population states were included: susceptible (S), infected (I), and recovered (R). It is assumed that these three populations are perfectly mixed and that everyone within the population has an equal probability of contacting each other person.

Infection occurs at a rate determined by $R_0$ and the infectious period $1/\gamma$, while infected individuals recover at rate $\gamma$. For this simple model, the transition between states can be described by the following set of ordinary differential equations

\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\diff S}{\diff t} = -\eqnmarkbox[uobblue]{inf1}{\frac{R_0\gamma SI}{N}}, 
    \qquad 
    \frac{\diff I}{\diff t} = \eqnmarkbox[uobblue]{inf2}{\frac{R_0\gamma SI}{N}} - \eqnmarkbox[uoborange]{rec1}{\gamma I}, 
    \qquad 
    \frac{\diff R}{\diff t} = \eqnmarkbox[uoborange]{rec2}{\gamma I}.
    \label{eq:SIR}
\end{equation}
\annotatetwo[yshift=-1em]{below}{inf1}{inf2}{Infection rate}
\annotatetwo[yshift=1em]{above}{rec1}{rec2}{Recovery rate}
\vspace{1.5em}

Since this model does not include births or deaths, $N = S + I + R$ is a fixed value. The values for $R_0$, $\gamma$, and $N$ are taken from the literature and are presented in Table~\ref{tab:model-params}. The proportion of the population initially susceptible to measles $S/N$, was taken to be 20\%. This basic SIR model is shown by the blue components of Figure~\ref{fig:SIR+isol}. 

However, the basic SIR model does not account for the isolation of individuals following identification of infection. This is an important omission when modelling measles outbreaks, as the identification and isolation of infectious individuals is a key component of outbreak management. An isolation mechanism was therefore incorporated into the extended model to allow the potential impact of outbreak response measures on transmission to be investigated.

One simple ways to model this is to implement an ``isolation mode" that turns on a fixed number of days after the first measles case. Once this mode is activated, a daily fixed proportion of infected persons are moved to an isolated state in which they can no longer infect others. The time delay to response $p_D$ and the isolation proportion $p_P$ are free model parameters. Their values will be calibrated to observed data as outlined in Section~\ref{sec:tuning}. When the ``isolation mode" is activated, the rate of change of infected persons is modified to be

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\diff I}{\diff t} \rightarrow \frac{R_0\gamma SI}{N} - \gamma I - \eqnmarkbox[uobgreen]{inf3}{p_P I}
    \label{eq:isolation}
\end{equation}
\annotate[yshift=-1em]{below}{inf3}{Isolation}
\vspace{1.5em}

Standard practice for communicable diseases is to only consider an outbreak ``over" after two maximum incubation periods occur without any new cases \cite{UKHSA2026MeaslesGuidelines}. Since the incubation period for measles is 7 to 18 days, the outbreak should be considered ongoing until 36 days have passed without a new case. After this point, authorities can stop looking for infected individuals.

This can be modelled with an ``isolation switch". The mechanics of this switch are demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:switch-model}. If there is at least one person currently infected, and the switch isn't currently on, then after a fixed delay $p_D$ the switch turns ``on" and a fixed proportion of infected persons are isolated each day. However, if there are no infected persons ($I(t)<1$), then the switch will turn off after two maximum incubation periods (36 days).

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[trim=10pt 250pt 480pt 10pt, clip=true, width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/model-diagrams/switch-diagram.pdf}
    \caption{Flow diagram outlining the mechanics of the measles isolation switch.}
    \label{fig:switch-model}
\end{figure}


Because the model represents population stocks as continuous quantities, an infected stock between zero and one would otherwise permit a fractional infectious population to generate further infections. To prevent this artefact, transmission was set to zero when the infected population fell below one individual. 

The additional simulation components related to this isolation mechanism are presented in green in Figure~\ref{fig:SIR+isol}.

\subsubsection{Base Model}

The base model, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:base-model}, is an extension of the SIR model with isolation to include three different age groups: babies ($<$ one year), children (1 to 4 years), and "adults" (5+ years), as well as an additional hospitalised population state. This gives a total of 15 different population states (3 age groups $\times$ 5 states). In this model, hospitalised individuals are assumed to also be isolated. Therefore, the addition of this state will change the isolation  delay ($p_D$) and proportion ($p_P$) needed to fit the model to observational data. The inclusion of a hospitalised state gives the model another output that can be compared and fitted to observed data. It is therefore necessary to include an additional free parameter $p_H$ which controls the rate of at which infected individuals are admitted to hospital. 

Importantly, the base model also includes childhood vaccinations. A simplifying assumption was made that those who receive a single dose of MMR become fully protected from measles infection. This assumption is justified since MMR is estimated to 92\% effective after just one dose \cite{10.1086/498902}. This vaccination is therefore modelled as movement into the vaccinated/recovered state, since vaccinated individuals are assumed to have equivalent protection against measles infection to recovered individuals. Since only children aged one to four years old are routinely vaccinated, only this group includes a path between the susceptible and recovered state in the base model. This number is taken to be the average number of vaccines given to children aged 1 to 4 years old in BSol between 2021 and 2025 \cite{NHSEngland2026FDP}. 

The model also includes age-specific migration into Birmingham with vaccination proportions estimated by combining migration estimates with WHO vaccination estimates as outlined in Appendix~\ref{app:model-params} \cite{ONS2011census, ONS2021census, WHO_measles_coverage}. However, importantly, the model does not account for migration out of BSol or internal migration to and from other areas within the UK. Finally, the model includes age-specific all-cause mortality calculated using data from the deaths register for deaths recorded between 2022 and 2025 \cite{BSolICBWarehouse2026}. This date range was chosen to avoid the impact of the Covid-19 pandemic on the mortality estimates.

\subsubsection{Age-specific rates}

The probability of infection and the probability of hospital admission when infected are age-dependent \cite{jary2025sociodemographic, van2020explaining}. Following the approach of \citeauthor{van2020explaining}, the basic reproduction number can be written as 
%
\begin{equation}
    R_0\gamma = \frac{\alpha_AP_A + \alpha_BP_B + \alpha_CP_C}{P_A + P_B + P_C},
    \label{eq:R0-age}
\end{equation}
%
where $\alpha_i$ and $P_i$ are the relative infection probability and population size respectively for adults (A), babies (B), and children (C) \cite{van2020explaining}. These parameters should be interpreted as effective age-specific transmission and hospital admission rates incorporating differences in exposure, susceptibility, and vulnerability rather than as estimates of biological susceptibility alone.

It can hence be calculated that

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:inf-rates}
    \frac{\alpha_B}{\alpha_A} = 1.99,
    \qquad 
    \frac{\alpha_C}{\alpha_A} = 4.90.
\end{equation}
%
Substituting into Equation~\ref{eq:R0-age}, it can be therefore be shown that $\alpha_A = 1.27$, $\alpha_B = 2.52$, and $\alpha_C = 6.20$. The age-specific infection rates can then be calculated by making the replacement $R_0 \gamma \rightarrow \alpha_i$.

Similarly, the observed ratio of hospital admissions among infected individuals (Table~\ref{tab:age-inf-rates}) was used to estimate relative age-specific hospitalisation rates
%
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:hosp-rates}
    h_B = 1.89 h_A,
    \qquad 
    h_C = 0.940h_A.
\end{equation}
%
However, to ensure that the modelled hospital admissions match the observed numbers, the infected adult daily hospitalisation proportion $h_A$ is left as a free parameter to be fitted.

The error function for weekly incidence $E_I$ and hospital admissions $E_H$ were then calculated using Equation~\ref{eq:error} for a range of values for the isolation delay $p_D$, isolation proportion $p_P$, and adult hospitalisation proportion $p_H$. From this, the combination of parameters that minimises the combined error function was calculated.

\subsubsection{Model Calibration}
\label{sec:tuning}

The model was calibrated using a grid-search approach, in which simulations were run for every combination of the free parameters across predefined parameter ranges. The daily model outputs were aggregated into weekly values and compared with observations from the large 2023/24 measles outbreak in BSol. Weekly laboratory-confirmed case data for Birmingham were extracted from \citeauthor{jary2025sociodemographic} \cite{jary2025sociodemographic}. Weekly data were not available for Solihull. A first-order approximation was therefore constructed by allocating the annual number of Solihull cases across weeks in proportion to the observed Birmingham epidemic curve (see Appendix~\ref{app:cases-approx}). Measles-related hospital admissions data were calculated using Secondary User Statistics (SUS) from the Birmingham and Solihull Integrated Care Board (BSol ICB) data warehouse. All dominant hospital episodes with an ICD-10 code starting with 'B05' (measles) were included.

Both models were initialised with a single infectious individual to represent the introduction of measles into an otherwise susceptible population. For the base model, this individual was assigned to the adult age group. For simplicity, the remaining population in the SIR model was initiated such that $S/N=0.9$. For the base model, the initial number of people in hospital or isolation was set to zero, and the number of people initially in the susceptible and recovered/vaccinated states were determined based on ONS census, NHS vaccination, and WHO vaccination data \cite{ONS2021census, NHSEngland2026FDP, WHO_measles_coverage}. This exact initial conditions are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:init-cond}.

To maximise the use of data, the model is tuned by minimising an error function. As the objective is to reproduce the outbreak dynamics rather than the calendar timing of the outbreak, a temporal alignment parameter, $\tau$, was applied during error calculation to allow the simulated epidemic to be shifted relative to the observed epidemic. For each parameter combination, the value of $\tau$ that minimised the error was selected. The error function is then given by
%
\begin{equation}
    E_V(\mathbf{p}, \tau) = \sum_t\left( V_{t+\tau}^{\text{sim}}(\mathbf{p}) - V_t^{\text{obs}}\right)^2 
    \label{eq:error}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathbf{p}$ represents all free model parameters to be tuned, and $V_{t+\tau}^{\text{sim}}$ and $V^{\text{obs}}$ are the simulated and observed outcomes values respectively. 

There are two observable values for the model to be calibrated to: measles infections and hospital admissions. Therefore, the full error function to be minimised is
%
\begin{equation}
    E(\mathbf{p}) = \frac{E_I(\mathbf{p})}{\mathrm{SD}(E_I)} + \omega \frac{E_H(\mathbf{p})}{\mathrm{SD}(E_H)}
    \label{eq:error-comb}
\end{equation}
%
where $E_I$ and $E_H$ are the error functions for infections and hospital admissions, respectively and $\omega$ determines the relative weighting assigned to hospital admission error $E_H$ compared with infection error $E_I$. The error functions are normalised by dividing by their standard deviation across all parameter combinations tested in the grid search. Since this investigation is most interested in measles infections, a value of $\omega = 0.5$ was chosen. It should be noted that choice for $\omega$ is subjective and that there is no ``correct" value. The impact of the choice of $\omega$ on the fitted calculated parameter values was investigated through sensitivity analysis.


\section{Results}

\subsection{Participatory System Modelling}
A participatory measles modelling workshop was held on the 20th of March 2026. This workshop, hosted by Birmingham City Council, brought together local experts to build a conceptual model of the local measles system, identify behavioural and epidemiological factors that facilitate or block flows in the system, and identify potential interventions to improve these flows. Attendees to the workshop included employees from Birmingham City Council (7), Birmingham and Solihull Integrated Care Board (2), and Solihull Metropolitan Council (1).

Due to the workshop's length, Copilot was used to extract audio contributions to supplement the written materials. The full output, along with all non-sensitive materials related to this project, can be viewed on  \href{https://github.com/David-Ellis/Measles-System-Dynamics-BSol}{GitHub}. 

\subsubsection{Barriers and Facilitators to Vaccination}

Workshop participants were separated into two groups and given a basic flow diagram for the local measles system with five connected population states: susceptible, vaccinated, infected, hospitalised, and recovered. The groups were asked to identify all the various factors influencing the rate of flow of people from one state to another. These factors were appended to the model at the flow point that they influence. The resulting models have been digitally transcribed and are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:trans-models} as Figures~\ref{fig:group1-model} and \ref{fig:group2-model}. Photographs of the models produced in the workshop can also be found on \href{https://github.com/David-Ellis/Measles-System-Dynamics-BSol}{GitHub}.

Once participants had collated all of the factors they could think of, they were asked to vote on which factors they deemed most important. Each participant was given three stickers with which they could vote on factors. These votes are also transcribed in Figures~\ref{fig:group1-model} and \ref{fig:group2-model}. After the workshop, these factors were grouped together and counted. The total number of votes for each flow factor is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:flow-factors}.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:flow-factors}, all of the factors voted on related to vaccination. The factor with the most votes was the accessibility of vaccination ($n=7$), followed by health literacy ($n=6$), the influence and previous experiences of family members ($n=4$), and community norms ($n=4$).

\subsubsection{Prioritised Interventions}

Participants were asked to review the models they had created and identify the ``problematic" flows within the system. In their groups, they were asked to brainstorm a list of potential interventions to improve vaccine uptake and which organisations they thought would be responsible for these interventions. These interventions are also shown in Figures~\ref{fig:group1-model} and \ref{fig:group2-model}.

The groups were then asked to each choose the three interventions that they thought were worth prioritising including in the model. These six interventions were then anonymously ranked by each of the workshop attendees using Mentimeter. The results were then aggregated using a Borda count approach, in which each ranked position was assigned a decreasing number of points (six points for rank one, five points for rank two, etc) and the total score for each option was used to generate the final ordering \cite{borda1784, taylor2005}. The total number of points for each intervention is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:int-voting}.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/workshop/intervention-voting.pdf}

    \label{fig:int-voting}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:int-voting}, the most highly ranked intervention was an extension of the vaccination offer. This intervention, delivered jointly by the NHS and local authorities (LA), would involve expanding the avenues through which children could be vaccinated, including community pharmacies (NHS), health visitors (LA), and nurseries and playgroups (NHS/LA).

The second most highly ranked interventions was a local communications campaign to challenge misinformation and improve health literacy. Participants recommended that, while this work would be commissioned by the local authorities and/or the NHS, it would involve local influencers or marketing agencies. Doing so would help to ensure that campaigns are engaging, culturally relevant, and separate from formal government branding. It was noted that social media content that is humorous and relatable tends to outperform formal health messaging and that this should be harnessed in countering misinformation.  

The third most highly ranked intervention was to provide a gelatine-free vaccine as standard. It was agreed that this would improve uptake by reducing confusion and removing the need for families to specifically request the gelatine-free option. This intervention would be led by the national government, the Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunisation, and the NHS.

\subsection{Outbreak Management}

Within the presented model, outbreak management is controlled by the isolation delay $p_D$ and isolation proportion $p_P$. By varying these values it is possible to estimate how different outbreak management scenarios changes the number of people estimated to be infected and hospitalised during an outbreak. 

Figure~\ref{fig:outbreak-management} shows the number of estimates cases and admissions associated with changes in the isolation delay and proportion parameters from those derived from fitting to data from the 23/24 outbreak shown in Table~\ref{tab:tuned-params}. The isolation delay was varied from 35.5 days (-36\%) to 75 days (+36\%), and the isolation proportion was varied from 0.0763 (-19\%) to 0.150 (60\%). 

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/results/outbreak-response.pdf}
    \caption{Estimates number of cases (left) and hospital admissions (right) with changing isolation delay and proportion parameters compared to the 23/24 outbreak.}

Figure~\ref{fig:outbreak-management} shows that a small reduction in isolation proportion could greatly increase the number measles cases. For example, a 20\% increase in the daily proportion of infected individuals being isolated results in 88 (-24.5\%) fewer cases and 32 (-24.5\%) fewer hospital admissions.

In contrast, a 20\% reduction in the isolation delay (11.6 days) results in 191.6 (-53.4\%) fewer cases and 65.4 (-53.3\%) fewer admissions. A 20\% increase in the isolation delay, however, results in 378.4 (105.6\%) more cases and 129.2 (105.3\%) more hospital admissions.



\subsection{Outbreak Preparation}

Given the calibrated model, it is also possible to predict what future measles outbreaks might look like given different levels of child vaccination. Figure~\ref{fig:outbreak-prep} shows the estimated number of measles infections and measles-related hospital admissions after one and five years as a function of changing relative number of MMR vaccinations. For 0\% change, the number of yearly vaccinations is fixed to the average annual number of vaccines given to children aged 1 to 4 years old within BSol between 2021 and 2025. 

For each  annual vaccination rate value simulated, the simulation was repeated for every combination of the model parameters value including their maximum and minimum uncertainty values presented in Table~\ref{tab:tuned-params} giving a total of 3 $\times$ 3 $\times$ 3 = 29 simulations per vaccination rate. The uncertainty range shown was then determined by calculating the maximum and minimum number of cases and admissions for each vaccination rate.

\vspace{-0.5em}
\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/results/outbreak-prep.pdf}
    \caption{Total number of estimated measles cases (top) and hospital admissions (bottom) over an outbreak occurring in one (red) or five (purple) years as a function of the number of yearly child MMR vaccinations distributed.}
    \label{fig:outbreak-prep}
\end{figure}

It is estimated that, at current birth and vaccination rates, the number of people that would be infected in an outbreak reduces over time. After one year, the estimated number of measles infections and hospital admissions are 179.6 (78.3, 567.7) and 61.9 (30.9, 158.1), respectively, which are 193.4 (51.8\%) and 62.7 (50.3\%) fewer than the simulated estimate for today (see Table~\ref{tab:summary-stat-comp}). After five years, the estimated number of infections and hospital admissions fall to 42.9 (23.9, 92.8) and 15.2 (9.8, 26.4), respectively. Under the assumptions of the current model, the projected number of infections declines over time at current vaccination rates. This suggests that population susceptibility may decline in the coming years; however, this should not be interpreted as evidence that BSol is on a trajectory towards measles elimination due to the geographic, ethnic, and socioeconomic heterogeneity in vaccination rates. 

However, Figure~\ref{fig:outbreak-prep} also shows how the number of estimated infections and hospital admissions rises rapidly with falling yearly MMR vaccinations. If yearly MMR vaccinations were reduced by 10\% (1,536) or 20\% (3,072) then the estimated number of infections after one year are estimated to be 295.9 (115.3, 1174.9) and 517.2 (174.9, 2949.5) respectively, and after five years to be 171.8 (73.7, 551.8) and 1028.1 (285.4, 6815.9) respectively. It can be seen from the larger uncertainty ranges, that the estimates relating to lower child vaccination rates are much more dependent on the specific model parameters. 

Lastly, as expected, increasing the number of yearly MMR vaccination is estimated to further reduce the number of infections and hospital admissions in the event of an outbreak. If yearly MMR vaccinations were increased by 10\% (1,536) or 20\% (3,072) then the estimated number of infections after one year are estimated to be 113.5 (54.2, 306.4) and 74.1 (38.3, 177.5), respectively, and after five years to be just 13.5 (9.1, 23.1) for a 10\% increase. For a 20\% increase, after five years, it is estimated that herd immunity is reached and no infections occur. 

It should be noted that these simulations assume that the susceptible persons are mixed homogeneously amongst the population. In reality, this is known not to be the case due to the cultural, demographic, and socioeconomic trends in vaccine hesitancy and vaccine access. Therefore, even if, at a population level, the BSol area has reached the threshold for ``herd immunity", this may not be true for all areas across the region, meaning that outbreaks are still possible.

\section{Discussion}

% General discussion opener

This study presents preliminary findings from an ongoing project to develop an epidemiological model of measles transmission and hospitalisation incorporating vaccination and outbreak management in collaboration with local stakeholders. The findings include stakeholders' views on the factors perceived to influence parents' decisions to vaccinate their children, including barriers, facilitators and potential interventions to improve uptake. The development of the base model is also presented, including how changes in child vaccine uptake and outbreak management affect predicted measles cases and hospital admissions.

\subsection{Participatory System Modelling}

% Workshop - flow factors

In the collaborative modelling workshop, local system experts identified the factors they thought played had the greatest relative importance in preventing and facilitating parents' willingness to vaccinate. The most highly voted individual factors were vaccine accessibility and health literacy, both with a similar number of votes (7 and 6 respectively). While ``information'' was the most highly voted theme (13 votes in total), accessibility received the most votes for an individual factor. This is notable because research on parents' willingness to vaccinate has focused heavily on access to information, with less attention given to other dimensions of accessibility such as availability, affordability, and proximity \cite{chisnall2024parents}. This This is consistent with a 2024 systematic review on the barriers and enablers within the health system to childhood MMR and HPV vaccination specifically amongst disadvantaged, minority, and underserved populations in middle and high income countries found that limited time, geographic distance, and difficulties navigating the healthcare system were all factors that hindered childhood vaccination \cite{essa2024understanding}.

These findings contrast much of the media and literature discourse around falling vaccination coverage has focused on explicit vaccine scepticism \cite{Stouts456}. Yet, a 2015 survey of 1,792 parents living in England found that only 2\% of respondents reported actually refusing vaccination for their child(ren) \cite{CAMPBELL20172979}. This suggests that outright refusal may account for only a proportion of children who remain unvaccinated. Furthermore, a comparison of English vaccination coverage with parental survey data and social media sentiment similarly found that the decline in childhood vaccination coverage between 2012/13 and 2018/19 was occurred alongside increasing vaccine confidence and decreasing exposure to anti-vaccination material \cite{edelstein2020keep}.

% Workshop - interventions

Stakeholders also nominated and voted on interventions that they thought should be prioritised for further investigation. The most highly voted intervention was a supplementary vaccine offer through community pharmacies, health visitors, nurseries, and playgroups, followed by a communications campaign with local influencers and/or marketing agency to challenge misinformation. These choices are broadly consistent with stakeholders' identification of accessibility and health literacy as important factors since an extended vaccine offer could directly increase accessibility and a communications campaign could help to improve health literacy and reduce the impact of misinformation. 

However, offering a gelatine-free vaccine by default was voted to be the third most highly prioritised intervention for investigation despite vaccine ingredients not being identified as an important factor influencing parents' willingness to vaccinate their children against measles. 
The discrepancy may indicate that stakeholders' prioritisation of interventions did not simply reflect the aggregate ranking of the barriers identified in the preceding exercise.  

Stakeholders also identified a potential challenge with offering a gelatine-free MMR vaccine as the default since, at the time of writing, UKHSA guidance only permits GP practices to order up to 10 doses of the gelatine-free vaccine per account per week \cite{UKHSA2025VaccineUpdate364}.  Addressing such supply-side constraints would require action beyond individual GP practices and may therefore require consideration of national vaccine procurement and supply arrangements.

\subsection{Model Fitting}

% General model fit?
The free parameters of the extended SIR model and base model were both calibrated to data from the 23/24 outbreak, as outlined in Section \ref{sec:tuning}. Both models produced weekly case estimates that were in strong agreement with the observed epidemic curve. The base model additionally showed strong agreement with the weekly number of measles-related hospital admissions. The base model underestimated the cumulative number of cases by around 10\%. However, the distribution of cases across the three modelled age groups was broadly consistent with observations. Age-dependent hospital admissions also closely matched observations, but were slightly low for babies.

The model was calibrated and assessed against the same outbreak from which its parameters were calibrated, meaning that its performance on independent outbreaks has not been assessed. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:measles-background}, the 23/24 outbreak resulted in many more cases than the three outbreaks preceding it. Potential explanations for the increased impact of the 23/24 outbreak include the large clusters of susceptible individuals in places like Birmingham following prolonged suboptimal vaccination coverage, potentially exacerbated by the disruptive impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on routine immunisations \cite{jary2025sociodemographic, UKHSA2024AnnualReport}. Therefore, rather than providing a generic representation of a measles outbreak, the model should be considered a representation of the transmission dynamics observed in \bsol during the 23/24 outbreak.


\subsection{Outbreak Management}
% Outbreak management

By varying the parameters of the model, estimates were made about how responding to an outbreak differently would impact the number of measles infections. The model predicted that if the proportion of infected people isolated each day fell, it could result in a big increase in infections, however, increasing the proportion isolated $p_P$ only produced a marginal reduction in cases. Meanwhile, reducing the delay in isolation $p_D$ was estimated to have a big impact in reducing the number of cases. 

This outbreak management modelling highlights that reducing the effective delay between outbreak onset and implementation of isolation (encoded in this model via $p_D$) has a greater potential for suppressing the overall outbreak than improving the effectiveness of the case-finding efforts (encoded in this model via $p_P$). This finding is consistent with a 2021 evidence-based review of the effectiveness of contact tracing for the mitigation and suppression of viral diseases, as well as a 2021 systematic review of contact tracing methods for controlling measles outbreaks in low and middle-income countries \cite{craig2021effectiveness, mbivnjo2022measles}. This also arises mathematically from the exponential nature of infectious disease \cite{zhang2024seir}. For these reason, the World Health Organisation recommends that to create surveillance system capable of eliminating measles and rubella, the early detection of outbreaks should be given the highest priority \cite{world2024eliminating}.

%Consider a single case of measles (basic reproduction number $R_0 = 13.5$) in a fully susceptible population. Each infectious generation increases the number of cases by more than an order of magnitude. After two generations, there are approximately $13.5^2 = 182$ cases. After four generations, there are approximately $13.5^4 = 33,215$ cases. While the \textit{effective} reproduction number is much smaller than 13.5, this highlights how quickly the number of infected persons can increase. Once there are many infectious people, any intervention becomes less effective.

However, it is important to note that $p_D$ encodes all of the delays in people being isolated. For example, as well as the time it takes for health protection teams to identify that an outbreak is taking place, it also include the time taken to identify contacts and communicate their exposure to them.

The National Measles Guidelines recommend using mass messaging such as email, text or letter \cite{UKHSA2026MeaslesGuidelines}. During the 23/24 outbreak, Birmingham Children's Hospital Emergency Department contacted patients that had potentially been expose to measles via letter. A retrospective study of the impact of the on the Department recommended that text messages should replace exposure letters to speed up communication \cite{HARRIS202541, 10.1093/jamia/ocac242}. Overall, a review should be conducted into the methods used by all part of the healthcare system to communicate exposure to highly communicable diseases such as measles. This review should then look for ways to reduce delays in communication. 

Additionally, the effective time to isolation can be reduced by increasing public awareness of the symptoms of measles. Such public awareness campaigns should focus on communities with the lowest MMR uptake. Doing so has a dual benefit. Firstly it increases the probability that cases are seen by health professionals as early as possible and thus reported to the local authority (as required by the Health Protection Regulations 2010 \cite{hpnr_2010}). Secondly, due to the success of vaccination programmes, vaccine preventable diseases like measles become less visible which can reduce the perceived risk from the disease \cite{Dube08082013}. Sharing materials to help parents identify measles symptoms because of the increasing outbreak frequency could help to challenge this belief.

% Outbreak preparation
\subsection{Outbreak Preparation}

Finally, the model was then used to investigate about how varying levels of child vaccination might impact the total number of cases and hospital admissions of future outbreaks. It was estimated that if the number of yearly child vaccinations remained at the average for the last five years, the number of cases in future outbreaks reduce over time. This finding, however, appears to be inconsistent with the fact that MMR coverage in BSol is well below the 95\% WHO target for measles elimination and falling \cite{FTips}. All else being equal, falling vaccination coverage should result in a susceptible population that goes over time meaning a greater number of infections during a subsequent outbreak. Furthermore, an additional 10\% of MMR vaccination administered over the last five years were given to individuals aged 5 years or older (see Appendix~\ref{app:model-params}). However, only child vaccinations have been included in the model, thus raising the level of inconsistency further. 

This seems to arise due to a mismatch between the modelled number of births and the modelled number of yearly vaccinations. The model uses a single birth rate (16,346 year$^{-1}$) determined from the 2021 ONS Census but the vaccination rate (15,360 year$^{-1}$) is calculated based on the data from 2021 to 2025 \cite{ONS2021census, NHSEngland2026FDP}. Based on these values, we would expect MMR vaccination coverage to tend towards 94\% over time. In contrast, only 86.2\% of the current cohort of children aged between 5 and 6 years living in \bsol are recorded as having received at least one dose of MMR. A major reason that the estimated future coverage is so high is because birth rates in \bsol have been falling over at least the past five years \cite{ONS2024LiveBirthsICB}. The model therefore combines a current annual birth estimate with a historical annual count of vaccination events, rather than modelling vaccination coverage within successive birth cohorts. This issue can potentially be rectified in future version of the model by modelling vaccination as a proportion of each eligible birth cohort, rather than a fixed annual number of vaccinations.   

However, irrespective of the potential overestimation of the future vaccine coverage, this analysis still provides some useful insights. The model predicts a non-linear relationship between the yearly number of child vaccinations and the estimated number of infections and hence hospital admissions during an outbreak. Increasing the number of vaccinations results in marginal reductions in infections while reductions in vaccinations results in comparatively larger increases in infections. As the outbreak occurs further into the future, this relationship becomes more non-linear. Small reductions in vaccination compound over successive birth cohorts, resulting in a progressively larger susceptible population and hence cases if an outbreak occurs. This finding is particularly important for illustrating the potential risk posed by further declines in child MMR vaccination rates.

\subsection{Next Steps}

In future versions of this model, once the vaccine coverage overestimation issue has been resolved, simulations of this type can be used to estimate the vaccination coverage required to prevent sustained measles transmission in \bsol. This could include an analysis of the impact of increasing catch-up vaccinations for those aged five years and older who have not received a dose of MMR.

As discussed, the current transmission model assumes a homogeneous mixing of the susceptible population \cite{bjornstad2020seirs}. However, since there are strong geographic, ethnic, and socioeconomic trends in vaccination, the susceptible population is likely to be spatially and demographically clustered \cite{jary2025sociodemographic}. Some of this heterogeneity can be accounted for by stratifying the model by ethnicity. Additionally, since vaccination coverage varies substantially between ethnic groups, outbreak dynamics may depend on which communities are initially infected. Studying transmission dynamics  could help health protection teams identify communities in which outbreaks are likely to have the greatest impact. These results be used to inform the targeting of vaccination and measles-awareness interventions towards communities at greatest risk.

Future versions of the model will also be expanded to include some of the key barriers and facilitators to MMR vaccination identified by local system experts in Figure~\ref{fig:flow-factors}. The system dynamics approach adopted allows for these factors to be represented as interacting variables within the model. However, assigning quantitative values to abstract constructs such as health literacy or misinformation presents a challenge. An additional challenge will be determining how these factors should be combined to represent parents' overall willingness to vaccinate. Estimating the current values for each of these factors and their relative influence on parents' willingness to vaccinate will require further reviews of the literature, empirical data where available, and additional stakeholder engagement. 

Furthermore, the most interventions prioritised by stakeholders will also be implemented into the model to investigate their potential impacts on overall and ethnic inequality in vaccination and measles infections and hospital admissions. Where these interventions are expected to affect barriers to vaccination differently for different population groups, these differences can be included in the model to assess whether they reduce or widen existing inequalities in vaccination and hence infections and hospital admissions. The modelled outcomes for these interventions could provide stakeholders with evidence about which interventions are likely to have the greatest population-level impact as well as which are most effective at reducing health inequalities. 

As discussed, the parameters for the model presented have been calibrated to data from the 23/24 outbreak in \bsol. The external validity of the model could be assessed and potentially improved by estimating its parameters using data from a range of different outbreaks. Simulations using the resulting range of parameter estimates could then be used to quantify how variation between outbreaks affects modelled outcomes, providing an additional source of uncertainty around future projections.

\subsection{Limitations}

The findings of this study should be interpreted in light of several limitations. 

The literature review summarised in Section~\ref{sec:vaccine-barriers} specifically focused on factors that may prevent parents from vaccinating their children and did not systematically consider factors that facilitate vaccination. As this literature was presented to stakeholders before and during the workshop, it may have biased participants to identify barriers rather than facilitators, potentially influencing the factors subsequently identified and prioritised.

As highlighted in Section \ref{sec:vaccine-barriers}, barriers to vaccination vary between population groups. Therefore, having a singular collective vote for the whole system does not capture this variation and and risks producing a one-size-fits-all representation of the determinants of vaccination uptake. A better approach might be to work with stakeholders to first segment the population into relevant groups and then identify the most important factors separately for each group.

Additionally, the process of voting on the ``most important'' factors may have been subject to several sources of potential bias, including the size and legibility of the handwriting on each sticky note and where they are placed. Given more time, a more comprehensive approach for eliciting stakeholders' views on the relative importance of the identified factors would be pairwise comparison. In this, stakeholders are presented with two factors at a time and asked to choose which is most important. The individual choices can then be aggregated into an overall ordering \cite{van2014does}. The challenge for this method is that for a moderate number of factors, the number of comparisons can quickly become very cumbersome. A less labour-intensive approach might be the Best–Worst Scaling method. This method presents stakeholders with a series of smaller subsets of factors and asks them to identify the most and least important factor within each subset. The resulting choices could then be analysed to estimate a latent importance score for each factor, providing a more systematic basis for ranking the factors to be included within the model \cite{hollin2022best}. 

The stakeholder workshop also involved a small number of local system experts with a majority working Birmingham City Council. In particular, no one working in primary or secondary care was included and no one from \ukhsa was able to attend. The results of the workshop can therefore not be assumed to represent the views of all professionals working in measles prevention and control. In addition, people directly affected by the factors under investigation, including parents, schools, and members of communities with lower MMR uptake, were not directly involved in the participatory modelling

There are also several limitations in the current model. For instance, the model assumes that hospitalised patients are effectively isolated and therefore do not contribute to onward measles transmission. However, during the 23/24 outbreak, Birmingham Children's Hospital Emergency Department identified in-hospital contacts for 95 of the 161 hospital-confirmed cases included in the study, resulting in 2397 exposure letters being sent \cite{HARRIS202541}. Although it is not known how many of these contacts subsequently developed clinical measles, this provides evidence that hospital settings may contribute to measles exposure and that assuming complete isolation of hospitalised cases may underestimate transmission. 

Additionally, while the model considers the inward migration of individuals to \bsol from abroad, it does not consider outward migration or internal migration to and from other areas of the UK. Internal migration is particularly important as it is known that more people enter \bsol from other areas of the UK than abroad \cite{nomis_census2021_od}. Future versions of the model should incorporate both internal migration into and out of \bsol, in addition to international migration. 

Furthermore, the model was calibrated against the same 2023/24 outbreak against which its performance was assessed. As a result, the close agreement between modelled and observed outcomes does not demonstrate external validity, and the model's ability to reproduce independent measles outbreaks remains untested.


\section{Conclusion}

This dissertation presents the early development and preliminary evaluation of a system dynamics model of measles vaccination, transmission, and hospitalisation in \bsol,drawing on local stakeholder knowledge and expertise to identify key barriers and facilitators to child vaccination and potential interventions to improve uptake. This model was then used to explore how varying levels of annual MMR vaccination and different outbreak management scenarios affect the estimated number of measles infections and hospital admissions.

Among the barriers and facilitators identified, stakeholders ranked vaccine accessibility and health literacy as the highest-priority individual factors for \bsol, while factors under the theme of ``information'' received the most votes overall. Stakeholders also recommended potential interventions to improve MMR uptake and voted to prioritise extending the delivery of the vaccine into community settings and targeted communications as interventions for further investigation using system dynamics modelling. 

The calibrated model reproduced the broad epidemic curve and pattern of hospital admissions with good agreement to observations. The age distributions of infections and hospital admissions were also broadly consistent, with some underestimation of the total number of cases and the number of hospital admissions for babies.

The investigation of different outbreak scenarios estimated that reducing the total effective delay between the start of an outbreak and the isolation of infected individuals could substantially reduce the number of cases and hospital admissions, while increasing the rate at which infected individuals are identified would result in more marginal reductions. Additionally, changing the yearly rate of childhood MMR vaccinations resulted in a non-linear effect on the resulting outbreak burden, with reductions in vaccination producing disproportionately larger increases in infections and hospitalisations. This effect became larger with longer periods of reduced vaccination before an outbreak.

However, the present model was calibrated and assessed against data from the same 2023/24 outbreak and therefore its external validity remains untested. It also does not represent the heterogeneous transmission dynamics associated with differences in vaccination coverage between population groups. Future development will therefore focus on validating the model against multiple outbreaks, improving the representation of population movement and childhood vaccination coverage, and stratifying the model by ethnicity to model differences in vaccination coverage and therefore susceptibility between population groups. The behavioural factors and potential interventions identified by stakeholders will also be incorporated and evaluated.

This study demonstrates the potential for a system dynamics model built in collaboration with local system experts to model population susceptibility, measles transmission, and hospital admission. Following further development, including incorporating the behavioural factors and potential interventions identified by stakeholders, such a model could provide a valuable decision-support tool for intervention planning and outbreak response.

%\subsection{Migration}
%\label{app:migration}

A relatively crude method was used to estimate the number of vaccinated and unvaccinated persons entering BSol from abroad. Firstly, the 2021 census was used to calculate the number of babies (aged $<$ 1 year) and children (aged 1-4 years) living in BSol from each major world region: Africa, Antarctica and Oceania, Europe (not UK), Middle East and Asia, The Americas and Caribbean \cite{ONS2011census, ONS2021census}. The counted babies had only one year to enter the country, while the children had four years. Therefore, the yearly migration numbers for babies and children where taken to be the number of babies and the number of children divided by four, respectively. 

It was then assumed that all babies were unvaccinated and that children were vaccinated at the same proportion as the WHO estimates for the percentage of the population from each region that have received at least one dose of measles-containing vaccine \cite{WHO_measles_coverage}. The region-specific estimates are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:migration}. From this, it is estimated that there are 226 babies and 882 children entering BSol from abroad each year, with 90.9\% of the children having received at least one measles-containing vaccine.

    \centering

    \caption{Estimated number of vaccinated (blue) and unvaccinated (red) babies (top) and children (bottom) migrating each year to BSol from each of the WHO regions \cite{ONS2011census, ONS2021census, WHO_measles_coverage}.}

The above estimate for child migration assumes that children migrated into the country at a consistent rate over the four years preceding the census. While this is a reasonable approximation over a four-year time scale, it is inappropriate to make the same assumption for all adults. Therefore, the "adult" migration rate was estimated by comparing the number of people aged five years or more who were born outside of the UK living in BSol in the 2011 and 2021 censuses \cite{ONS2011census, ONS2021census}. From this, it is estimated that 6305.2 adults migrate into BSol each year. It is then assumed that the current pattern of migration origins for adults matches that of children and that therefore 90.9\% of these adults are vaccinated. 

These estimates should should reflect the rough ball-park figures for migration into \bsol. However, importantly, they do not account for migration out of \bsol or for internal migration to and from other areas within the UK. The latter is the most significant omission, as it is known that more people enter \bsol from other areas of the UK than abroad \cite{nomis_census2021_od}. Future models should take this additional incoming population source into account.
