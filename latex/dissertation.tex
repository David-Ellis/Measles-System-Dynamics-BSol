\subsection{Measles Epidemiology}
Measles is a highly infectious viral disease that can cause serious complications and death \cite{WHO_measles_topic}.
In high-income countries, complications occur in around 10-20\% of cases, and the case-fatality ratio is around 0.05\% \cite{wolfson2009estimates, NICE2024MeaslesComplications}. Clinically vulnerable groups, such as immunosuppressed individuals, infants under one year, and pregnant people, are more likely to develop complications \cite{NHSEngland2024MeaslesGuidance}.

\subsection{Measles in England and BSol}

The first measles vaccine was licensed in the US in 1963 \cite{MOSS20172490}. The measles vaccine was first introduced in the UK in 1968 and later replaced by the combined measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine in 1988 \cite{ukhsa_vaccination_timeline_1796_present}. As of 1 January 2026, varicella has been added to make the MMRV vaccine\footnote{For brevity, MMR and MMRV will simply be referred to as MMR(V).}. In the UK, all children are offered a first dose of MMR when they turn 12 months and a second dose at 18 months \cite{UKHSA2023MeaslesRubellaElimination}. The World Health Organisation (WHO) estimates that between 2000 and 2024, the measles vaccine has prevented  59 million deaths \cite{mondiale2025weekly}.

The UK achieved measles elimination in 2016 \cite{UKHSA2023MeaslesRubellaElimination}. However, measles outbreaks are becoming increasingly common in the UK and globally due to falling vaccine coverage \cite{minta2023progress}. The top half of Figure~\ref{fig:measles-background} shows that there has been a series of measles outbreaks in England since January 2012.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/background/measles-background.pdf}
    \caption{ (Top) Monthly lab-confirmed cases of measles in England \cite{UKHSA2025MMRLabConfirmedCasesQ3}. (Bottom) Yearly MMR coverage for one dose at two years old \cite{FTips}.}
    \label{fig:measles-background}
\end{figure}

The bottom half of Figure~\ref{fig:measles-background} shows these outbreaks occurring alongside falling national vaccination coverage from a peak of 92.7\% in 2013 down to 88.9\% in 2024.  However, in Birmingham and Solihull (BSol), the  coverage started lower and has fallen further than the England average from a peak of 89.7\% in 2014 down to 81.3\% in 2024 \cite{FTips}. This has contributed to the region suffering large measles outbreaks \cite{jary2025sociodemographic}.



Part of the reason for the falling MMR coverage is the growing scepticism in vaccines \cite{gschwend2026shadow, torracinta2021mmr}. While this is a trend for most vaccines, there has been particular public concern about the MMR vaccine due to the infamous, since retracted, claim by one research group that the MMR vaccine might cause autism \cite{novilla2023parents, wakefield1998retracted, godlee2011wakefield}. However, there are driving vaccine hesitancy that vary across population groups, including misinformation, health literacy, and lack of trust in the healthcare system \cite{cagnotta2025parental}. Some groups, such as Gypsy, Roma, and Traveller populations, also face additional barriers to accessing the vaccine \cite{wilder2020resurgence}. As a result, significant ethnic and socioeconomic inequality has been observed in MMR coverage \cite{Flatte079550, zhang2023ethnic}.

During the outbreak in Birmingham from 13 October 2023, the measles infection rate was 3.4 times higher for those living in the most deprived quintile compared to those living in the third most deprived quintile. No infections occurred in the least deprived quintile. Additionally, the infection rate for Black African residents was eight times greater than the White British population \cite{Tabacchi02072016}. Figure~\ref{fig:BSol-mmr-eth} shows that, except for those of Indian and Chinese heritage, children of all ethnic groups have lower MMR coverage at one dose than the White British cohort. The Black African and Black Caribbean have particularly low coverage at 74.2\% and 64.0\% respectively compared to 92.9\% for Whit British \cite{NHSEngland2026FDP}.

    \label{fig:BSol-mmr-eth}
\end{figure}
Among the Core Cities of England and Wales\footnote{England and Wales Core Cities include Birmingham, Bristol, Cardiff, Leeds, Liverpool, Manchester, Newcastle, Nottingham, and Sheffield.}, Birmingham has the highest level of ethnic diversity. Data from the 2021 Census show that just under half (48.5\%) of BSol residents identified as White British, meaning that a majority (51.5\%) reported a different ethnic background. This is almost double the England average of 26.5\%. The largest ethnic minority groups were Pakistani (14.9\%), Indian (5.7\%), Black African (5.0\%), and Other White (3.8\%) \cite{ONS2021census}.

All six WHO regions have declared measles elimination goals \cite{WHO_measles_topic}. The UK Measles and Rubella elimination strategy 2019 set the MMR vaccination target, based on WHO recommendation, to 95\% of two doses by 5 years of age in order to achieve and maintain measles elimination \cite{public2019uk}. The Public Health England (PHE) immunisation inequalities local action plan template outlines that factors responsible for inequitable vaccine uptake are context-specific \cite{PHE2021ImmunisationInequalities}. Therefore, in order to reverse the falling measles vaccine coverage and growing health inequalities, it is essential to understand the range of factors influencing parents’ decisions to vaccinate their children and how these factors are influenced by ethnicity.

\subsection{Barriers to Vaccination}
\label{sec:vaccine-barriers}

A rapid literature review was performed to inform the development of this project\footnote{The literature search strategy and full literature list can be found in dissertation protocol presented on the project's \href{https://github.com/David-Ellis/Measles-System-Dynamics-BSol/blob/main/pdfs/Measles\%20System\%20Dynamics\%20Project\%20Protocol.pdf}{GitHub repository}.}.

% Vaccine hesitancy review
A 2024 meta-analysis estimated the global prevalence of caregiver vaccine hesitancy to be 32\% but only 2\% for England \cite{maamor2024prevalence}. This England estimate, based on a small sample taken in 2015, does not seem consistent with the current level of MMR coverage \cite{CAMPBELL20172979}. Another 2017 systematic review of socioeconomic differences in childhood vaccinations found MMR uptake in England to be lower amongst more affluent parents \cite{bocquier2017socioeconomic}. Again, the most recent study this finding was based on was published in 2011. In contrast, ONS data shows that MMR uptake is lowest in the most deprived local authorities \cite{FTips}.

% Local evidence
Community engagement workshops conducted in Birmingham and Solihull, targeted at low-uptake ethnic groups, found similar overarching themes \cite{bissett_measles_soft_intelligence_2024, palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024, smbc_community_engagement_learning_2025}. Language barriers and low reading ability arose as a key issue. Many parents in BSol speak little English and therefore struggle to benefit from English-only flyers while others are unable to read in any language. This highlights the importance of using multiple media forms, including video, for outreach \cite{palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024, smbc_community_engagement_learning_2025}. Participants also reported that the materials used to promote vaccination lacked diversity and did not represent the communities they were from \cite{palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024}. This finding mirrors that of a 2024 systematic review on the barriers and enablers to MMR uptake among disadvantaged, minority, or underserved populations \cite{essa2024understanding}.

Concerns about the ingredients and safety of the vaccine varied by community. For example, Muslim participants were very concerned about pork gelatine and the option of a pork-free vaccine \cite{palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024, smbc_community_engagement_learning_2025}. This again mirrors global research where the most commonly cited religiously motivated barriers to measles vaccination were animal-derived gelatins used in the vaccine production and the use of aborted human fetus tissue to make the rubella component of the MMR vaccine \cite{wombwell2015religious}.

Additionally, for Muslim/Afro-Caribbean communities, fears about possible links to autism were also seen to be important, while these were less important for the Romani traveller community \cite{palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024, smbc_community_engagement_learning_2025}. This local diversity in opinion is important as a 2023 systematic review found the risk of autism to be the most commonly cited concern about the vaccine \cite{novilla2023parents}.

There were also issues raised about the accessibility of the vaccine services, particularly for migrants and asylum seekers
\cite{bissett_measles_soft_intelligence_2024}. A survey of support staff working with unaccompanied asylum-seeking children (UASC) living in Birmingham found that UASC often arrive with missing vaccination records, and that there often isn’t enough time within appointments to give the required immunisations \cite{kemishi_uasc_health_wellbeing_2025}. Additionally, a 2025 review found that many migrants living in Europe don’t know how to access vaccine services and don’t know what they’re eligible for, including fears of costs \cite{halliday2025vaccine}. This is a particularly unnecessary barrier since all migrants in the UK, including those with No Recourse to Public Funds (NRPF), are eligible for free vaccinations on the NHS \cite{nrpf_nhs_treatment_overview, ukhsa_migrant_immunisation_2022}. The same review found that first-generation immigrants had low vaccination rates and high levels of unknown vaccination status, reflecting local evidence \cite{halliday2025vaccine}. In particular, one 2020 Welsh study found that asylum-seeking children had a 3 times reduced odds of being vaccinated compared to the general population \cite{perry2020inequalities}. A 2019 systematic review of parental attitudes and beliefs of the measles vaccine also found that parents from Gypsy, Roma, and Traveller populations faced unique accessibility barriers due to their transient residency as well as regular discrimination and exclusion within the healthcare system \cite{wilder2020resurgence}.

Participants in the Birmingham engagement workshops also raised the issue of trust \cite{bissett_measles_soft_intelligence_2024, palmer_birmingham_measles_engagement_2024}. Some participants reported a general mistrust of government sources, particularly if associated with the ‘Home Office’ and ‘immigration’. A general preference was raised for information to come from healthcare professionals and local community/faith leaders. A lack of trust in the healthcare system has also been found to be a barrier to vaccination in the UK \cite{torracinta2021mmr}.

Other reasons for vaccine hesitancy include a belief that children are given too many vaccines too soon, and a low perceived benefit of vaccination \cite{novilla2023parents}. A 2025 cross-sectional study in Türkiye also found parents were 2.5 times more likely to exhibit vaccine hesitancy if one of their children had already experienced adverse effects from vaccination \cite{komurluouglu2025vaccine}.

\subsection{Rationale and Aims}

The evidence outlined above suggests that parents' willingness to have their children vaccinated against measles is influenced by a complex combination of systemic, sociological, and psychological factors. The combined impact of these factors may produce non-linear behaviour within the measles system, including feedback loops and delays, which can be challenging to represent using simpler linear or compartmental approaches \cite{bjornstad2020seirs, rodriguez2025heuristic}. Some methods, including neural networks, can model complex non-linear behaviour but may act as a ``black box'' and provide less insight into how and why the system behaves as it does \cite{anwar2025stochastic, burkart2021survey}. In contrast, system dynamics provides a modelling framework that allows for the study of complex systems including the non-linear interactions between different system elements \cite{bala2017system}. System dynamics can also represent comparatively abstract and difficult-to-quantify factors such as ``trust in the vaccine'' and ``level of health literacy'' \cite{van2020explaining}. Therefore, due to its flexibility and ability to study behavioural factors, a system dynamics approach will be used.

This dissertation presents a snapshot of an ongoing piece of work led by \bcc in collaboration with stakeholders from \solihull, \bsolicb, and \ukhsa. The findings from this project will contribute to future work packages for the Birmingham and Solihull Measles Elimination Group \cite{ukhsa_measles_briefing_2026}.

The overarching project aim is to develop a system dynamics model to support decision-making in collaboration with a range of local health protection experts working in BSol. The intended full model will incorporate ethnicity-dependent behavioural and epidemiological factors, identified by stakeholders through a series of interactive workshops, that facilitate and block flows in the system. The model will also simulate potential interventions to improve these flows. 

This dissertation presents the results from the first interactive workshop including most important factors blocking and facilitating parents' willingness to vaccinate their children and the interventions that stakeholders prioritised for further investigation as potential responses to declining childhood MMR(V) vaccination coverage in \bsol. The development of the base measles model. This is an age-dependent model of measles transmission, vaccination, and hospital admission. Preliminary predictions on the impact of modified outbreak preparation and response are presented. 

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Stakeholder Engagement}

\tkDE{Measles is complicated.} Therefore, stakeholders with expertise of different aspects of the measles system were invited to participate in a participatory modelling workshop. Organisations were invited to nominate delegates to participate. These organisations included \bsolicb, \ukhsa, and the public health teams from \bcc, and \solihull. Attempts were also made to ensure that a range of skill sets were present at the workshop including measles case management, child vaccinations, data analytics, and behavioural science. 

To promote full participation, stakeholders were divided into two groups, with group composition selected to provide a broad range of backgrounds and expertise. A simplified version of the stakeholder engagement approach used by \citeauthor{michail2025evaluation} was employed \cite{michail2025evaluation}.

Prior to the workshop, participants were provided with an evidence pack outlining the rationale for using system dynamics and summarising local evidence on the systemic, sociological, and psychological barriers to childhood vaccination described in Section~\ref{sec:vaccine-barriers}. The workshop was held at Birmingham City Council House and was facilitated with support from the BCC Governance Team. Participant contributions were recorded using paper and sticky notes, with all materials photographed and subsequently transcribed.

The workshop was divided into three parts. First, participants were given a high-level overview of the evidence pack and an introduction to system dynamics. A practical model-building exercise using flows of money into and out of a bank account was then used to introduce the core concepts of system dynamics.

Second, participants were presented with a simplified measles model showing the principal stocks and flows within the system. For each key flow, participants identified factors that could facilitate or block the flow using sticky notes. Participants then voted on the factors they considered most influential using a limited number of stickers. This was used to focus subsequent model development on factors considered most influential to system behaviour.

Finally, participants identified flows that they considered could be improved and proposed potential policies and interventions to alter these flows. For each intervention, participants identified the organisation responsible, the location within the system at which it would act, and the expected direction of its effect on the relevant flow. These contributions were also recorded using sticky notes. Participants subsequently used an anonymous online voting platform to rank the proposed interventions according to their perceived feasibility and impactability. The resulting prioritisation was used to identify the interventions on which subsequent modelling would focus.

\subsubsection{SIR model}
\label{sec:sir}

To first simulate the most basic system behaviour, only three population states were included: susceptible (S), infected (I), and recovered (R). It is assumed that these three populations are perfectly mixed and that everyone within the population has an equal probability of contacting each other person.

The three population states are connected by an infection rate determined by the basic reproduction number $R_0$, the infectious period $1/\gamma$, and the recovery rate, which is also determined by the infectious period. For this simple model, the transition between states can be described by the following set of ordinary differential equations

\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\diff S}{\diff t} = -\eqnmarkbox[blue]{inf1}{\frac{R_0\gamma SI}{N}}, 
    \qquad 
    \frac{\diff I}{\diff t} = \eqnmarkbox[blue]{inf2}{\frac{R_0\gamma SI}{N}} - \eqnmarkbox[magenta]{rec1}{\gamma I}, 
    \qquad 
    \frac{\diff R}{\diff t} = \eqnmarkbox[magenta]{rec2}{\gamma I}.
    \label{eq:SIR}
\end{equation}
\annotatetwo[yshift=-1em]{below}{inf1}{inf2}{Infection rate}
\annotatetwo[yshift=1em]{above}{rec1}{rec2}{Recovery rate}
\vspace{1.5em}

Since this model does not include births or deaths, $N = S + I + R$ is a fixed value. The values for $R_0$, $\gamma$, and $N$ are taken from the literature and are presented in Table~\ref{tab:model-params}. The proportion of the population initially susceptible to measles $S/N$, was taken to be 20\%. This basic SIR model is shown by the blue components of Figure~\ref{fig:SIR+isol}. 

If there exists one or more infected persons ($I>0$), more people will be infected so long as $\diff I / \diff t >0$. Therefore, from Equation~\ref{eq:SIR}, it can be seen that the condition for an outbreak in an SIR model is that, 
%
\begin{equation}
    \frac{S}{N} = \frac{1}{R_0}.
\end{equation}
%
This is the SIR condition for herd immunity. For measles, $R_0=13.5$. Therefore, the SIR model predicts that an outbreak will continue until there are fewer than 7.5\% susceptible to infection. 

In reality, measles outbreaks are not left to infect people until herd immunity is achieved through natural infection. Instead, authorities such as the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) intervene to break the spread of transmission by identifying and isolating infected individuals. A model that reproduces observed outcomes therefore requires a mechanism to model this isolation process. 

One simple ways to model this is to implement an ``isolation mode" that turns on a fixed number of days after the first measles case. Once this mode is activated, a daily fixed proportion of infected persons are moved to an isolated state in which they can no longer infect others. The time delay to response $p_D$ and the isolation proportion $p_P$ are free model parameters. Their values will be tuned to observed data as outlined in Section~\ref{sec:tuning}. When the ``isolation mode" is activated, the rate of change of infected persons is modified to be

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\diff I}{\diff t} \rightarrow \frac{R_0\gamma SI}{N} - \gamma I - \eqnmarkbox[red]{inf3}{p_P I}
    \label{eq:isolation}
\end{equation}
\annotate[yshift=-1em]{below}{inf3}{Isolation}
\vspace{1.5em}

Standard practice for communicable diseases is to only consider an outbreak ``over" after two maximum incubation periods occur without any new cases \cite{UKHSA2026MeaslesGuidelines}. Since the incubation period for measles is 7 to 18 days, the outbreak should be considered ongoing until 36 days have passed without a new case. After this point, authorities can stop looking for infected individuals.

This can be modelled with an ``isolation switch". The mechanics of this switch are demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:switch-model}. If there is at least one person currently infected, and the switch isn't currently on, then after a fixed delay $p_D$ the switch turns ``on" and a fixed proportion of infected persons are isolated each day. However, if there are no infected persons ($I(t)<1$), then the switch will turn off after two maximum incubation periods (36 days).

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[trim=10pt 250pt 480pt 10pt, clip=true, width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/model-diagrams/switch-diagram.pdf}
    \caption{Flow diagram outlining the mechanics of the measles isolation switch.}
    \label{fig:switch-model}
\end{figure}

Since the simulation deals in continuous values rather than discrete units representing individual people, it is necessary to modify the number of new cases to be

\begin{equation}
    \text{Infection rate} = \begin{cases}
        R_0 \gamma S  I/ N, & \text{if } I \geq 1,\\
        0, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
% 
This means that new infections can only occur if the number of people infected is equal to or greater than one. This prevents the unrealistic case of new outbreaks occurring due to less than one infected person. 

The additional simulation components related to this isolation mechanism are presented in green in Figure~\ref{fig:SIR+isol}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/model-diagrams/SIR+isolation.png}
    \caption{Diagram of a basic SIR system (blue) extended to include the ability to isolate infected individuals (green) at a fixed daily proportion $p_P$. This isolation mode is activated $p_D$ days after the first infection has occurred.}
    \label{fig:SIR+isol}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Base Model}

The full model, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:full-model}, is an extension of the SIR model with isolation to include three different age groups: babies ($<$ one year), children (1 to 4 years), and "adults" (5+ years), as well as an additional hospitalised population state. This gives a total of 15 different population states (3 age groups $\times$ 5 states). In this model, hospitalised individuals are assumed to also be isolated. Therefore, the addition of this state will change the isolation threshold ($p_T$) and proportion ($p_P$) needed to fit the model to observational data. 

The inclusion of a hospitalised state gives the model another output that can be compared and fitted to observed data. It is therefore necessary to include an additional free parameter $p_H$ which controls the rate of at which infected individuals are admitted to hospital. 

Importantly, the full model also includes childhood vaccinations. 
Vaccination is modelled as movement into the vaccinated/recovered state, since vaccinated individuals are assumed to have equivalent protection against measles infection to recovered individuals. Since only children aged one to four years old are routinely vaccinated, only this group includes a path between the susceptible and recovered state in the base model. This number is taken to be the average number of vaccines given to children aged 1 to 4 years old in BSol between 2021 and 2025 \cite{NHSEngland2026FDP}. It also includes age-specific migration into Birmingham with vaccination proportions estimated by combining migration estimates calculated from the 2011 and 2021 censuses with WHO vaccination estimates as outlined in Appendix~\ref{app:migration} \cite{ONS2011census, ONS2021census, WHO_measles_coverage}. However, importantly, the model does not account for migration out of BSol or internal migration to and from other areas within the UK. Finally, the model includes age-specific all-cause mortality calculated using data from the deaths register for deaths recorded between 2022 and 2025 \cite{BSolICBWarehouse2026}. This date range was chosen to avoid the impact of the Covid-19 pandemic on the mortality estimates.

\subsubsection{Age-specific rates}

The probability of infection and the probability of hospital admission when infected are age-dependent \cite{jary2025sociodemographic, van2020explaining}. Following the approach of \citeauthor{van2020explaining}, the basic reproduction number can be written as 
%
\begin{equation}
    R_0\gamma = \frac{\alpha_AP_A + \alpha_BP_B + \alpha_CP_C}{P_A + P_B + P_C},
    \label{eq:R0-age}
\end{equation}
%
where $\alpha_i$ and $P_i$ are the relative infection probability and population size respectively for adults (A), babies (B), and children (C) \cite{van2020explaining}.

The number of infections per 1000 persons not vaccinated with at least one dose of MMR are presented in Table~\ref{tab:age-inf-rates} for each age group. From this, it can be deduced that

\begin{equation}

    \qquad 
    \frac{\alpha_C}{\alpha_A} = 4.90.
\end{equation}
%
Substituting into Equation~\ref{eq:R0-age}, it can be therefore be shown that $\alpha_A = 1.27$, $\alpha_B = 2.52$, and $\alpha_c = 6.20$. The age-specific infection rates can then be calculated by making the replacement $R_o \gamma \rightarrow \alpha_i$.

Similarly, from Table~\ref{tab:age-inf-rates}, it can be shown that the hospital admission rates of infected babies and children are given by 
%
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:hosp-rates}
    h_B = 1.89 h_A,
    \qquad 
    h_C = 0.940h_A.
\end{equation}
%
However, to ensure that the modelled hospital admissions match the observed numbers, the infected adult daily hospitalisation proportion $h_A$ is left as a free parameter to be fitted.

When fitting the free parameters of the extended full model, the system was initialised with a single infected ''adult". The error function for weekly incidence $E_I$ and hospital admissions $E_H$ were then calculated using Equation~\ref{eq:error} for a range of values for the isolation delay $p_D$, isolation proportion $p_P$, and adult hospitalisation proportion $p_H$. From this, the combination of parameters that minimises the combined error function was calculated.

These parameters should be interpreted as effective age-specific transmission and hospital admission rates incorporating differences in exposure, susceptibility, and vulnerability rather than as estimates of biological susceptibility alone.

\subsubsection{Model tuning}
\label{sec:tuning}
To maximise the use of data, the model is tuned by minimising an error function. As the objective is to reproduce the outbreak dynamics rather than the calendar timing of the outbreak, a time-lag parameter, $\tau$, is included so that simulated outcomes are compared with observed outcomes at the corresponding point in the epidemic. The error function is then given by
%
\begin{equation}
    E_V(p_i, \tau) = \sum_t\left( V_{t+\tau}^{\text{sim}}(p_i) - V_t^{\text{obs}}\right)^2 
    \label{eq:error}
\end{equation}
%
where $p_i$ represents all free model parameters to be tuned, and $V_{t+\tau}^{\text{sim}}$ and $V^{\text{obs}}$ are the simulated and observed measurable values respectively. 

There are two observable values for the model to be tuned to: measles infections and hospital admissions. Therefore, the full error function to be minimised is
%
\begin{equation}
    E(p_i) = \frac{E_I(p_i)}{\mathrm{sd}(E_I)} + \omega \frac{E_H(p_i)}{\mathrm{sd}(E_H)}
    \label{eq:error-comb}
\end{equation}
%
where $E_I$ and $E_H$ are the error functions for infections and hospital admissions, respectively and $\omega$ is a weighting parameter that encodes the relative importance of $E_I$ to $E_H$ in the error function calculation. The error functions $E_I$ and $E_H$ are normalised by dividing by their standard deviation across all parameters. Since this investigation is most interested in measles infections, a value of $\omega = 0.5$ was chosen. It should be noted that choice for $\omega$ is subjective and that there is no ``correct" value. However, as outlined in Appendix~\ref{app:omega-sense}, the derived free parameter values vary only moderately with changing $\omega$. \tkDE{Is this last line a result? - I think so.}

\subsubsection{Data}

Model parameters are tuned to data from a large measles outbreak that occurred from late 2023 to 2024. Weekly lab-confirmed cases for Birmingham were extracted from \citeauthor{jary2025sociodemographic} \cite{jary2025sociodemographic}. Weekly case data for Solihull were not available. Therefore, a first order approximation was made using yearly data from the UK Health Security Agency \cite{ukhsa_measles_2023_2026}. Measles-related hospital admissions data were calculated using Secondary User Statistics (SUS) from the Birmingham and Solihull Integrated Care Board (BSol ICB) data warehouse. All dominant hospital episodes with an ICD-10 code starting with 'B05' were included. 


\section{Results}

\subsection{Stakeholder Engagement}
A participatory measles modelling workshop was held on the 20th of March 2026. This workshop, hosted by Birmingham City Council, brought together local experts to build a conceptual model of the local measles system, identify behavioural and epidemiological factors that facilitate or block flows in the system, and identify potential interventions to improve these flows. Attendees to the workshop included employees from Birmingham City Council (7), Birmingham and Solihull Integrated Care Board (2), and Solihull Metropolitan Council (1).

Due to the workshop's length, Copilot was used to extract audio contributions to supplement the written materials. The full output, along with all non-sensitive materials related to this project, can be viewed on  \href{https://github.com/David-Ellis/Measles-System-Dynamics-BSol}{GitHub}. 

\subsubsection{Barriers and Facilitators to Vaccination}

Workshop participants were separated into two groups and given a basic flow diagram for the local measles system with five connected population states: susceptible, vaccinated, infected, hospitalised, and recovered. The groups were asked to identify all the various factors influencing the rate of flow of people from one state to another. These factors were appended to the model at the flow point that they influence. The resulting models have been digitally transcribed and are presented in Appendix~\ref{app:trans-models} as Figures~\ref{fig:group1-model} and \ref{fig:group2-model}. Each participant was given three stickers with which they could vote on factors. These votes are also transcribed in Figures~\ref{fig:group1-model} and \ref{fig:group2-model}. After the workshop, these factors were grouped together and counted. The total number of votes for each flow factor is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:flow-factors}.

\subsubsection{Prioritised Interventions}

Participants were asked to review the models they had created and identify the ``problematic" flows within the system. These six interventions were then anonymously ranked by each of the workshop attendees using Mentimeter. The results were then aggregated using a Borda count approach, in which each ranked position was assigned a decreasing number of points and the total score for each option was used to generate the final ordering \cite{borda1784, taylor2005}. The total number of points for each intervention is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:int-voting}.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:int-voting}, the most highly ranked intervention was an extension of the vaccination offer. The second most highly ranked interventions was a local communications campaign to challenge misinformation and improve health literacy. This intervention would be led by the national government, the Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunisation, and the NHS.

\subsection{Outbreak Management}

Within the presented model, outbreak management is controlled by the isolation delay $p_D$ and isolation proportion $p_P$. By varying these values it is possible to estimate how different outbreak management scenarios changes the number of people estimated to be infected and hospitalised during an outbreak. 

Figure~\ref{fig:outbreak-management} shows the number of estimates cases and admissions associated with changes in the isolation delay and proportion parameters from those derived from fitting to data from the 23/24 outbreak shown in Table~\ref{tab:tuned-params}. The isolation delay was varied from 35.5 days (-36\%) to 75 days (+36\%), and the isolation proportion was varied from 0.0763 (-19\%) to 0.150 (60\%). 

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/results/outbreak-response.pdf}
    \caption{Estimates number of cases (left) and hospital admissions (right) with changing isolation delay and proportion parameters compared to the 23/24 outbreak.}
    \label{fig:outbreak-management}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:outbreak-management} shows that a small reduction in isolation proportion could greatly increase the number measles cases. For example, a 20\% decrease in the daily proportion of infected individuals being isolated results in 300.3 (+83.8\%) more cases and 102.6 (+83.6\%) more hospital admissions. However, increasing the isolation proportion provides diminishing returns on improvements in outcomes. For example, a 20\% increase in the daily proportion of infected individuals being isolated results in 88 (-24.5\%) fewer cases and 32 (-24.5\%) fewer hospital admissions.

In contrast, a 20\% reduction in the isolation delay (11.6 days) results in 191.6 (-53.4\%) fewer cases and 65.4 (-53.3\%) fewer admissions. A 20\% increase in the isolation delay, however, results in 378.4 (105.6\%) more cases and 129.2 (105.3\%) more hospital admissions.



\subsection{Outbreak Preparation}

Given the tuned model, it is also possible to predict what future measles outbreaks might look like given different levels of child vaccination. Figure~\ref{fig:outbreak-prep} shows the estimated number of measles infections and measles-related hospital admissions after one and five years as a function of changing relative number of MMR(V) vaccinations. The shaded regions represent the region of parameter space within 10\% of the minimum model error. For 0\% change, the number of yearly vaccinations is fixed to the average annual number of vaccines given to children aged 1 to 4 years old within BSol between 2021 and 2025. 

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/results/outbreak-prep.pdf}
    \caption{Total number of estimated measles cases (top) and hospital admissions (bottom) over an outbreak occurring in one (red) or five (purple) years as a function of the number of yearly child MMR(V) vaccinations distributed.}
    \label{fig:outbreak-prep}
\end{figure}

It is estimated that, at current birth and vaccination rates, the number of people that would be infected in an outbreak reduces over time. After one year, the estimated number of measles infections and hospital admissions are 179.6 (78.3, 567.7) and 61.9 (30.9, 158.1), respectively, which are 193.4 (51.8\%) and 62.7 (50.3\%) fewer than the simulated estimate for today (see Table~\ref{tab:summary-stat-comp}). After five years, the estimated number of infections and hospital admissions fall to 42.9 (23.9, 92.8) and 15.2 (9.8, 26.4), respectively. Under the assumptions of the current model, the projected number of infections declines over time at current vaccination rates. This suggests that population susceptibility may decline in the coming years; however, this should not be interpreted as evidence that BSol is on a trajectory towards measles elimination due to the geographic, ethnic, and socioeconomic heterogeneity in vaccination rates. 

However, Figure~\ref{fig:outbreak-prep} also shows how the number of estimated infections and hospital admissions rises rapidly with falling yearly MMR(V) vaccinations. If yearly MMR(V) vaccinations were reduced by 10\% (1,536) or 20\% (3,072) then the estimated number of infections after one year are estimated to be 295.9 (115.3, 1174.9) and 517.2 (174.9, 2949.5) respectively, and after five years to be 171.8 (73.7, 551.8) and 1028.1 (285.4, 6815.9) respectively. It can be seen from the larger uncertainty ranges, that the estimates relating to lower child vaccination rates are much more dependent on the specific model parameters. 

Lastly, as expected, increasing the number of yearly MMR(V) vaccination is estimated to further reduce the number of infections and hospital admissions in the event of an outbreak. If yearly MMR(V) vaccinations were increased by 10\% (1,536) or 20\% (3,072) then the estimated number of infections after one year are estimated to be 113.5 (54.2, 306.4) and 74.1 (38.3, 177.5), respectively, and after five years to be just 13.5 (9.1, 23.1) for a 10\% increase. For a 20\% increase, after five years, it is estimated that herd immunity is reached and no infections occur. 

It should be noted that these simulations assume that the susceptible persons are mixed homogeneously amongst the population. In reality, this is known not to be the case due to the cultural, demographic, and socioeconomic trends in vaccine hesitancy and vaccine access. Therefore, even if, at a population level, the BSol area has reached the threshold for ``herd immunity", this may not be true for all areas across the region, meaning that outbreaks are still possible.

\section{Discussion}

% Outbreak management
This analysis highlights that reducing the delay between outbreak onset and implementation of isolation (encoded in this model via $p_D$) has a greater potential for suppressing the overall outbreak than improving the effectiveness of the case-finding efforts (encoded in this model via $p_P$). This finding is consistent with a 2021 evidence-based review of the effectiveness of contact tracing for the mitigation and suppression of viral diseases, as well as a 2021 systematic review of contact tracing methods for controlling measles outbreaks in low and middle-income countries \cite{craig2021effectiveness, mbivnjo2022measles}. 

However, this also arises mathematically from the exponential nature of infectious disease \cite{zhang2024seir}. Consider a single case of measles (basic reproduction number $R_0 = 13.5$) in a fully susceptible population. Each infectious generation increases the number of cases by more than an order of magnitude. After two generations, there are approximately $13.5^2 = 182$ cases. After four generations, there are approximately $13.5^4 = 33,215$ cases. While the \textit{effective} reproduction number is much smaller than 13.5, this highlights how quickly the number of infected persons can increase. Once there are many infectious people, any intervention becomes less effective.

For these reason, the World Health Organisation recommends that to create surveillance system capable of eliminating measles and rubella, the early detection of outbreaks should be given the highest priority \cite{world2024eliminating}.

\subsection{Migration}
\label{app:migration}

A relatively crude method was used to estimate the number of vaccinated and unvaccinated persons entering BSol from abroad. Firstly, the 2021 census was used to calculate the number of babies (aged $<$ 1 year) and children (aged 1-4 years) living in BSol from each major world region: Africa, Antarctica and Oceania, Europe (not UK), Middle East and Asia, The Americas and Caribbean \cite{ONS2011census, ONS2021census}. The counted babies had only one year to enter the country, while the children had four years. Therefore, the yearly migration numbers for babies and children where taken to be the number of babies and the number of children divided by four, respectively. 

It was then assumed that all babies were unvaccinated and that children were vaccinated at the same proportion as the WHO estimates for the percentage of the population from each region that have received at least one dose of measles-containing vaccine \cite{WHO_measles_coverage}. The region-specific estimates are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:migration}. From this, it is estimated that there are 226 babies and 882 children entering BSol from abroad each year, with 90.9\% of the children having received at least one measles-containing vaccine.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figures/model-params/migration-estimates.pdf}
    \caption{Estimated number of vaccinated (blue) and unvaccinated (red) babies (top) and children (bottom) migrating each year to BSol from each of the WHO regions \cite{ONS2011census, ONS2021census, WHO_measles_coverage}.}
    \label{fig:migration}
\end{figure}

The above estimate for child migration assumes that children migrated into the country at a consistent rate over the four years preceding the census. While this is a reasonable approximation over a four-year time scale, it is inappropriate to make the same assumption for all adults. Therefore, the "adult" migration rate was estimated by comparing the number of people aged five years or more who were born outside of the UK living in BSol in the 2011 and 2021 censuses \cite{ONS2011census, ONS2021census}. From this, it is estimated that 6305.2 adults migrate into BSol each year. It is then assumed that the current pattern of migration origins for adults matches that of children and that therefore 90.9\% of these adults are vaccinated. 

These estimates should should reflect the rough ball-park figures for migration into BSol. However, importantly, they do not account for migration out of BSol or for internal migration to and from other areas within the UK. The latter is the most significant omission, as it is known that more people enter BSol from other areas of the UK than abroad \cite{nomis_census2021_od}. Future models should take this additional incoming population source into account.
